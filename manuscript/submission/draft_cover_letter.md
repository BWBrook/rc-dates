I am pleased to submit our manuscript, *Within-site sampling influences radiocarbon “dates-as-data” demographic reconstructions*, for consideration as an Original Research Article in the *Journal of Archaeological Science*.

Radiocarbon “dates-as-data” approaches are now widely used to reconstruct past human demography. Most methodological work has focused on calibration, aggregation, smoothing, taphonomy and variation in research intensity. Our manuscript tests an earlier and less quantified source of bias: which contexts within each site are selected for dating, and how many determinations each site contributes. Archaeological dating is rarely exhaustive, and a regional dataset can remain biased as it grows if many sites contribute only their oldest, youngest or single dated context.

We examine this problem through an empirical-simulation study based on the AustArch database for Sahul. We first impose random-1, random-5, oldest, youngest and bracketed down-sampling rules on a well-dated Holocene reference set and measure how they reshape SPDs, KDEs and frequency distributions. We then use the empirical site structure in simulations where the generating population trajectory is known, adding random-3 and quantifying model-recovery accuracy, parameter bias and unambiguous model support. Population-growth models are fitted directly to calibrated radiocarbon posteriors and compared using AIC, so the inferential analysis retains chronological uncertainty without treating a smoothed aggregate curve as population history.

The main findings are:

* Within-site sampling has a larger effect on inference than the choice among SPD, KDE and FD summaries once curves are normalised and aligned.
* Rules that preserve temporal span within sites, including all dates and random-*n* strategies using three or more dates, produce high model recovery, clearer support and negligible parameter bias.
* Oldest, youngest and random-1 rules reduce model-recovery accuracy, weaken support and induce directional parameter bias.

The practical consequence is direct: where exhaustive dating is impossible, field programmes should prioritise determinations that span the occupational sequence; databases should record per-site date counts, stratigraphic span and selection criteria; and regional analyses should test sensitivity to sparsely dated sites. The manuscript is therefore a methods paper with broad archaeological application, combining empirical analysis, formal modelling and simulation to improve the reliability of scientific inference. We consider it well matched to the scope of *Journal of Archaeological Science*.
