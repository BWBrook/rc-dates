# Within-site sampling influences radiocarbon “dates-as-data” demographic reconstructions

<div id="authors"></div>

_Word count:_ ~6,000 (Abstract to Discussion)

## Abstract

Radiocarbon “dates-as-data” is widely used to investigate past demographic and environmental change. Although many methodological issues have been examined over the last decade, the impact of within-site sampling decisions remains poorly quantified. Here we combine a Holocene subset of the AustArch database with simulations to measure how common archaeological field sampling at site level might affect summed probability distributions (SPD), kernel density estimates (KDE), and frequency distributions (FD) used for visualisation and demographic inference. We then apply a suite of date-subsampling patterns to either 50% or 100% of sites and use these results to parameterise simulations of synthetic population trajectories. Five within-site subsampling rules were used: all dates, random-*n* (n = 1, 3 or 5 dates), oldest, youngest, and bracketed (oldest + youngest). Simulations generated radiocarbon determinations under three growth models (uniform, linear, exponential), with site counts, per-site sample sizes, and age–error structure based on the dataset. Inference used likelihood-based methods fitted to calibrated posteriors, with model choice by Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC). Performance was assessed by model-recovery accuracy, parameter bias, and evidential strength (ΔAIC after accounting for estimation uncertainty). The results show that: i) the all-dates and random-*n* strategies with n ≥ 3 consistently yield high model recovery (~0.9), negligible bias in growth parameters, and strong information-theoretic support, largely independent of intensity; ii) oldest, youngest, and random-1 rules degrade accuracy (~0.3–0.4), parameter estimates show large, directional biases, and ΔAIC margins shrink; and iii) once curves are normalised and aligned, differences among SPD, KDE, and FD are secondary to within-site sampling. We conclude that, given real-world constraints, sampling strategies that preserve as much temporal span within sites as possible are more informative than those using random-1, oldest, or youngest rules. When exhaustive dating is impossible, at least three well-spaced samples spanning the within-site sequence should be the minimum target for broader radiocarbon modelling. We also recommend publishing information on within-site parameters used in radiocarbon modelling to support interrogation of bias and uncertainty.

## Keywords

radiocarbon dating; archaeological demography; within-site sampling; dates-as-data; summed probability distributions; likelihood-based inference; simulation; Sahul.

## Highlights

- We quantify how within-site sampling rules reshape radiocarbon “dates-as-data” demography using an AustArch-parameterised empirical–simulation design.

- Once curves are normalised, within-site sampling dominates smoother choice (SPD/KDE/FD) for accuracy, bias, and ΔAIC support.

- Span-preserving strategies (all dates; random-*n* with n ≥ 3) produce high model recovery, negligible parameter bias, and strong evidential strength.

- The bracketed rule (oldest + youngest) aids model identification but induces predictable directional bias, while oldest, youngest, and random-1 rules perform poorly.

- We introduce a likelihood-based inferential framework operating directly on calibrated posteriors, yielding reproducible guidance for field design and analysis.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 “Dates as data” for reconstructing past human population change

Archaeologists increasingly treat radiocarbon determinations as data for population inference, aggregating them to track temporal change in human activity and, by proxy, population size [@rick1987DatesDataExamination; @robinson2019SpatiotemporalDynamicsPrehistoric; @ulm1996RadiocarbonCulturalChronologies; @williams2016RadiometricDatesAre]. The rationale is that, all else being equal, intervals with more dated activity represent intervals with more people doing datable things. This logic has been applied from European Neolithic booms and busts [@shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @timpson2014ReconstructingRegionalPopulation] to Holocene dynamics in Sahul [@johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient; @williams2013NewPopulationCurve; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon] and other regions [@brown2019MaoriPopulationGrowth; @chaput2016RadiocarbonDatesEstimates; @porcic2016DemographyEarlyNeolithic] and, more recently, to continental and supra-regional syntheses [e.g., @wirtz2024MulticentennialCyclesContinental]. Radiocarbon sample abundance has also been used to test this dates-as-data proxy for population size against independent estimates, and to track non-human dynamics, including late Holocene Californian populations and predator–prey systems [@bird2020FirstEmpiricalAnalysis; @codding2024CountsRadiocarbondatedArchaeological; @marom2024NotePredatorpreyDynamics; @roalkvam2025ComparingSummedProbability]. Several analytical approaches are in common use. Summed probability distributions (SPD) remain the default [@crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon], while fixed-bin frequency distributions (FD [@brown2015FilterDarklyPopulation; @michczynska2007FrequencyDistributionRadiocarbon; @surovell2007NoteUseTemporal]), kernel density estimates (KDE [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @mendez-quiros2023ModelingLongtermHuman]), and more explicit population or occupancy models [@johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient; @robinson2019SpatiotemporalDynamicsPrehistoric] are also used.

Several biases affect these approaches. Physical processes cause differential preservation of older archaeological material, producing the familiar “pull of the recent” [@bluhm2019ValidationGlobalModel; @surovell2009CorrectingTemporalFrequency]. Research effort varies across space and time, and recent rescaling work shows that inter-settlement differences in sampling intensity can change SPD-based reconstructions [@park2026RescalingRadiocarbonData]. The radiocarbon calibration curve introduces non-Gaussian structure that can be aliased by binning and smoothing [@carleton2021SumThingsAre; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @ramsey2024DevelopmentINTCALDatabase]. Correctives such as taphonomic adjustments [@peros2010PrehistoricDemographyNorth; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon], binning protocols [@williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon], and simulation-based tests [@shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @williams2015HoloceneDemographicChanges] have therefore become standard. Recent work has also argued that SPDs themselves lack a clear statistical definition and has explored alternatives that treat dated events as count or Poisson processes rather than as smoothed probability densities [@crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @hanna2025SumPoissonApproach; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon]. A further source of distortion sits closer to dataset formation in archaeological practice: which contexts within each site are dated, and how many samples are collected [@williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. This study examines that source of distortion and its effects on radiocarbon data proxies.

### 1.2 Overview of common analytical approaches and biases

Budgets, research aims, sample availability, and preservation constraints mean that archaeologists rarely date every context at a site. Sampling intensity is therefore highly variable and constrained by site-specific and budgetary factors, and it is still common for only a few samples to be collected from any given site. This constraint is amplified where archaeological fieldwork is undertaken through cultural resource management or development-led contract work, where compliance goals, project timelines, and fixed budgets can favour targeted minimal dating over exhaustive site chronology [@codding2024CountsRadiocarbondatedArchaeological]. While there are no specific rules for sampling, it is common to “date the oldest layer to anchor the sequence”, “date the youngest to bracket occupation”, “pick one representative hearth”, or “take two dates, oldest and youngest” [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @price2021EndtoendBayesianAnalysis].

This pattern is visible in radiocarbon databases: single-date sites are commonplace, and two-date sites are often used to bracket occupancy [@saktura2023SahulArchGeochronologicalDatabase; @williams2014AustArchDatabase14C; @williams2013NewPopulationCurve]. It is particularly marked in legacy datasets compiled from mid- and late-twentieth century investigations, when radiocarbon dating was less available, although the number of dates per site has increased in recent projects as AMS determinations have become cheaper and more routine. Such choices can skew SPDs or other aggregators [@contreras2014SummedRadiocarbonCalibrations; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon], but the magnitude of within-site sampling effects, and their interaction with downstream analytic choices, remains under-quantified even in recent model-based work. If many sites contribute only their oldest or youngest context, regional curves can be shifted earlier or later; if one-date samples dominate, within-site variance is suppressed. Since these decisions are heterogeneous and often undocumented, their influence is hard to separate from taphonomy and research intensity in observational datasets.

We address this gap by combining an empirical Holocene reference set from the AustArch radiocarbon database of Sahul [@williams2014AustArchDatabase14C] with controlled simulations to isolate the inferential consequences of within-site sampling regimes. From AustArch we quantify typical site-level date counts, temporal spans and age–error structure, and show how common within-site rules reshape aggregate SPDs, KDEs and FDs. We then embed these empirically derived patterns in a large simulation study where the true population trajectories are known, applying alternative within-site rules to dates drawn from simple growth models and fitting them with population-growth likelihoods to assess model recovery, parameter bias and evidential strength.

A key methodological distinction underpins the analysis. Rather than first smoothing all dates into a regional curve and interpreting that curve as population history, we keep the uncertainty attached to each radiocarbon determination. Each calibrated date is treated as a range of possible calendar ages, with some ages more probable than others. We then ask which simple population-growth model would make those dates most likely. Model performance is summarised with maximum likelihood and Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) [@akaike1974NewLookStatistical], which balance fit against model complexity. We report three outcomes: whether the true model is recovered, whether the estimated growth parameter is biased, and how strongly the data support the preferred model. SPDs, KDEs and FDs are retained as descriptive plots. In this respect our approach sits alongside recent Poisson and count-based frameworks that model radiocarbon sample occurrence directly [@crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @hanna2025SumPoissonApproach; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon], but it targets a different source of distortion: the within-site rules that determine which contexts enter those models in the first place.

The paper is methodological in scope. It does not attempt to reconstruct Australian population history or correct for taphonomy or variable research intensity, which are well documented and important [@bluhm2019ValidationGlobalModel; @peros2010PrehistoricDemographyNorth; @surovell2009CorrectingTemporalFrequency; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon; @williams2013NewPopulationCurve]. The focus is within-site selection, which has been discussed previously but has not yet been robustly measured. By anchoring simulations in AustArch and calibrating with contemporary curves, we provide practical guidance for future work, complementing recent probabilistic frameworks for radiocarbon-driven palaeodemography [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon].

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 AustArch data analysis

#### 2.1.1 Study context and empirical reference set

Our aim was to quantify how decisions on the within-site temporal distribution of radiocarbon dates, whether deliberate or inadvertent, distort regional “dates-as-data” inference. To anchor the analysis in realistic site structures we parameterised the study from AustArch [@williams2014AustArchDatabase14C], a continental-scale compilation for Sahul (5,044 determinations from 1,748 sites). To reduce long-term taphonomic contrast while retaining a large regional dataset, we constrained the analysis to Holocene data through three phases of selection.

1. Initial screening. We retained radiocarbon determinations (both conventional and AMS) with reported ages and one-sigma errors; records flagged as erroneous or unrelated to human activity were removed. Ages were initially restricted to 100–20,000 $^{14}$C yr BP so that all possible Holocene observations lay within the SHCal20 or Marine20 calibration ranges [@heaton2020Marine20TheMarineRadiocarbon; @hogg2020SHCal20SouthernHemisphere; @ramsey2024DevelopmentINTCALDatabase; @reimer2020INTCAL20NorthernHemisphere].

2. Material and context filtering. To reduce laboratory and reservoir idiosyncrasies we accepted charcoal/wood, human bone collagen, and well-attested freshwater or marine shells from clearly anthropogenic contexts (e.g., rock shelters, middens, hearths, earth mounds). Ambiguous contexts and “unusable” designations were excluded. Duplicate laboratory codes indexing the same context/material were combined when compatible ($\chi^2$ test) using rcarbon::combine.

3. Holocene restriction and site eligibility. Dates were first calibrated and then truncated to the Holocene window 11,650-651 cal BP; the upper bound avoids near-modern boundary effects. We kept only sites contributing ≥5 valid Holocene determinations, yielding a final reference set of 976 determinations from 116 sites (median 7.5 dates per site). This threshold excludes sparsely dated sites, including those with fewer than three determinations, from the empirical baseline because they do not contain enough within-site chronological information to benchmark what a sampling rule has removed. Their consequences are instead assessed experimentally by down-sampling the better-dated sites into sparse-site analogues, including random-1, random-3, oldest, youngest, and bracketed scenarios. Weighted-mean calibrated ages were computed for descriptive plots only; all inference used the full calibrated posteriors.

All processing was done in R 4.5.1 [@rcoreteam2025LanguageEnvironmentStatistical], using the tidyverse for data handling and rcarbon [@crema2021InferenceLargeSets] for calibration. Random seeds were set at the start of each Monte Carlo block for reproducibility.

#### 2.1.2 Baseline demographic curves

The curated AustArch set defines an “all-dates” baseline (Fig. 1) for comparison with sub-sampling scenarios (Fig. 2). We constructed three familiar summaries on a common 100-yr calendar grid:

1. Summed probability distribution (SPD). Calibrated posterior grids were summed and then smoothed with a 100-yr running mean.

2. Frequency distribution (FD). Simple counts in 100-yr bins were computed using each date’s median calibrated age.

3. Kernel density estimate (KDE). A Gaussian KDE (bandwidth 100 yr) was centred on each median calibrated age.

Each curve was normalised to unit area; for joint display the SPD and KDE were re-binned to the FD grid to avoid visual aliasing. These curves provide descriptive context only and do not enter the likelihood-based inference.

#### 2.1.3 Within-site sub-sampling experiments

To mimic common field and budget constraints, we imposed five within-site rules on the AustArch reference set: i) random-1: one random determination per treated site; ii) random-5: five random determinations per treated site (see Fig. 2); iii) oldest: the determination with the earliest median calibrated age; iv) youngest: the determination with the latest median calibrated age; v) bracketed: the pair consisting of the oldest and youngest determinations.

Each rule was applied at two intensities: 50% of sites treated (with the remainder contributing all dates) or 100% treated; the all-dates baseline (Fig. 1) is equivalent to 0% in this framing. For stochastic rules we ran 1,000 Monte Carlo realisations per scenario. For deterministic rules, randomness enters only via the 50% site subset; we also generated 1,000 draws of that subset.

A single realisation comprised: i) sampling the treated sites (if intensity < 100%); ii) selecting date indices within treated sites according to the rule; iii) appending all dates from untreated sites; iv) calibrating determinations with SHCal20 (terrestrial) or Marine20 (shells) (for the purposes of simulation no regional marine offset, or $\Delta R$, was applied in calibrating marine dates); and v) computing SPD, FD, and KDE curves and re-normalising to unit area. For every rule × intensity combination we calculated, at each calendar bin, the mean and 2.5th/97.5th percentiles across realisations. Discrepancy from the all-dates baseline SPD was summarised using `rcarbon::compare_spds`. Figures plot the baseline together with the Monte Carlo mean and 95% interval.

These empirical experiments are used diagnostically to show how within-site rules deform regional aggregates, rather than as demographic reconstructions.

### 2.2 Simulation study

#### 2.2.1 Sample-size and age-error models based on empirical data

To ensure our simulations reflected realistic archaeological dating scenarios, we used the cleaned and filtered AustArch dataset (section 2.1) to parameterise key aspects of radiocarbon sampling. We fitted a truncated negative binomial distribution (minimum 5 dates per site) to the distribution of sample sizes across archaeological sites, capturing the typical variation in dating intensity between sites. We also performed linear regression of logged standard errors against median calibrated radiocarbon ages to model how measurement uncertainty varies with sample age.

#### 2.2.2 Growth models, synthetic-data generation, and sampling strategies

For the Holocene-restricted date range (11650-651 cal BP), we defined three population growth models as unit normalised, truncated probability densities: constant (uniform), linearly increasing, and exponentially increasing. Unit normalisation completely determines the uniform density, and it constrains the truncated linear and exponential distributions to one-parameter models which can be uniquely characterised by specifying the proportionate increase in population over the restricted date range.

Each synthetic radiocarbon dataset was characterised by the choice of growth model. For the linear and exponential models, we fixed their distributions by specifying a 10-fold population increase, close to the Holocene increase found by @johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient. To simulate a dataset, we drew a set of site sample sizes from the negative binomial sample-size model, and calibrated ages from the probability distribution of the specified growth model. To ensure median calibrated dates would lie in the restricted date range, we uncalibrated these ages, simulated standard errors from the age–error model, then recalibrated the dates using rcarbon.

We evaluated seven sampling strategies across three levels of bias intensity (50%, 75%, or 100% of sites). The strategies comprised: all dates (using every available radiocarbon determination), random-1, random-3, and random-5 (selecting dates randomly without replacement from each site), oldest (selecting the oldest date from each site), youngest, and bracketed (selecting both oldest and youngest dates from each site). Sites not selected for the biased sampling strategy (i.e., at the 50% and 75% intensities) contributed all their dates.

#### 2.2.3 Likelihood calculation, model fitting and model selection

Rather than using smoothed representations of the data such as kernel densities or regressions that do not respect unit normalisation, we fitted the normalised population growth distributions directly to the calibrated dates. Given the distributional nature of the calibrated dates, we considered two possible definitions for the pointwise log-likelihood $\ell_i$:

$$
\ell_i^1 = \log \int f(x \mid \theta)\, p_i(x)\, dx
$$

and

$$
\ell_i^2 = \int \log\!\bigl(f(x \mid \theta)\bigr)\, p_i(x)\, dx
$$

where $f(x \mid \theta)$ is the probability density of the growth model, $p_i(x)$ is the posterior probability density of the calibrated age, and $\theta$ is the distributional parameter to be estimated. The $\ell^1$ approach is more commonly used for response-error distributions, while $\ell^2$ is often used to compute information criteria [@gelman2013BayesianDataAnalysis]. In practice, the calibrated age distribution $p_i(x)$ is represented as a discrete set of values, and the integrals are replaced by finite sums. The $\ell^2$ approach is equivalent to fitting models directly to SPD curves, where each discrete probability value under the growth model is weighted by its corresponding SPD density and the total number of discrete points.

We fitted all models by maximum likelihood estimation using each definition of the log likelihood. We then computed the Akaike Information Criterion ($\mathrm{AIC} = -2\ell + 2k$), where $\ell = \sum_{i=1}^N \ell_i$ is the total log likelihood of the fitted growth model and $k$ is the number of estimated parameters [@akaike1974NewLookStatistical], and applied the modified one-standard-error (OSE) rule to identify models that have comparable AIC estimates while accounting explicitly for estimation uncertainty [@yates2021ParsimoniousModelSelection]. Specifically, the OSE rule selects models with $\Delta \mathrm{AIC} \le \mathrm{SE}(\Delta \mathrm{AIC})$, where $\Delta \mathrm{AIC}$ is the difference from the minimum AIC and $\mathrm{SE}(\Delta \mathrm{AIC})$ is the standard error of the pointwise $\Delta \mathrm{AIC}$ values across sites.

@timpson2021DirectlyModellingPopulation propose a likelihood equivalent to our $\ell^1$, although they use BIC rather than AIC for model comparison. They argue against using SPD curves as input data for models; our results show that equivalent outcomes are obtained when the contributing points are appropriately weighted (our $\ell^2$).

#### 2.2.4 Simulation design and performance evaluation

We generated 1,000 simulated datasets for each of the three population models (uniform, linear, exponential). Each dataset was then subsampled using all sampling strategies and bias intensities, resulting in 63,000 total analysis scenarios. For every subsampled dataset, we fitted all three candidate models using both likelihood approaches and applied both minimum AIC and OSE model selection rules.

We evaluated sampling strategy performance using three metrics: i) *accuracy* – the proportion of simulations correctly identifying the true population model, ii) *parameter bias* – the difference between estimated and true model parameters for linear and exponential models, and iii) *evidential strength* – the proportion of simulations where the OSE rule selected only the true model without ambiguity. Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) was used to generate confidence intervals for accuracy estimates.

### 2.3 Reproducibility

All code, random seeds, and intermediate results (site subsets, index matrices, re-binned summaries, simulation outputs) are archived in a Zenodo release and GitHub repository, enabling full reproducibility checks without re-running calibration. High-volume simulation runs were parallelised on institutional high-performance computing.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Empirical behaviour of the AustArch reference set

Figure 1 shows the 0% sub-sampling baseline: all eligible determinations calibrated with SHCal20 or Marine20, then summarised as SPD, KDE, and FD curves on a common 100-year grid. At centennial resolution, the three aggregators agree closely. The FD is jagged by construction, the KDE is slightly smoother, and the smoothed SPD tracks the KDE. The curves show low and noisy early-mid Holocene values, a gradual rise through the mid-to-late Holocene, and a steep increase in the last two millennia. Since each curve is normalised to unit area, these displays describe relative density only and serve as a visual reference for the sub-sampling experiments.

Figure 2 illustrates how plausible within-site sampling rules deform this baseline when applied to 50% or 100% of sites. Choosing the oldest determination per site converts the dataset toward a distribution of site onsets. At 100% intensity the FD develops step-like mid-Holocene plateaus and the SPD/KDE shift mass from the most recent centuries into earlier periods; at 50% intensity these effects are diluted by the untreated half of sites. Selecting the youngest determination produces the mirror distortion. With 100% intensity the very recent peak is attenuated, and structure is redistributed into the mid-to-late Holocene; at 50% the curves track the baseline more closely but still show a damped recent amplitude.

The bracketed rule (oldest + youngest) preserves site spans but reweights probability toward their edges. Even when applied to every site, the overall shape remains similar to the baseline, yet the very late Holocene mass is only modestly elevated relative to mid-span levels. The random-1 rule (one random determination per treated site) is approximately unbiased in expectation but volatile: Monte Carlo ribbons widen across the Holocene, especially with 100% sampling intensity when every site is reduced to a single date. The random-5 rule preserves span while capping per-site influence; the resulting curves remain close to the baseline and uncertainty ribbons are narrow. Across all panels, differences among SPD, KDE, and FD are minor compared with the effect of the within-site rule. These results motivate the simulation analysis.

### 3.2 Simulation experiments

All simulation results reported here use the $\ell^1$ likelihood; the $\ell^2$ variant yields qualitatively identical patterns (Supplementary Information). Figures 3–5 report model recovery, parameter bias, and evidential strength for each within-site sampling rule and intensity.

#### 3.2.1 Model recovery

Model recovery is summarised in Figure 3. Span-preserving strategies perform strongly and are insensitive to intensity. Using all dates or random-5 recovers the correct family in roughly 0.92–0.96 of replicates across 50%, 75%, and 100% application. Random-3 performs slightly worse but remains high and stable. Reducing each treated site to one random date (random-1) leads to a monotonic decline: performance near 0.95 when only half of sites are affected falls into the mid-0.8s when every site is reduced to one date.

The oldest and youngest rules fare poorly. The oldest rule appears accurate at low intensity but collapses as the rule is applied to more sites, falling to roughly one-third correct at 100%. The youngest rule performs poorly at all intensities, with accuracy around 0.4 and even worse performance as more sites are subjected to the rule. The bracketed rule is mid-tier (~0.65) and largely flat across intensities: it clearly outperforms either oldest or youngest alone, but it does not match the span-preserving random-sampling strategies.

#### 3.2.2 Parameter bias

Parameter estimation gives a sharper view of these differences (Fig. 4). When the generating model is exponential, the growth rate is effectively unbiased under all dates and random-*n*. The oldest and youngest rules show strong, sign-opposed biases that increase with intensity: oldest underestimates the rate (negative bias), youngest overestimates it (positive bias), and bracketed inherits a milder averaged negative bias. Under linear truth, slopes behave similarly on a tighter scale.

#### 3.2.3 Evidential strength

The evidential-strength results distinguish high model recovery from unambiguous model support (Fig. 5). The all-dates rule is near 0.9 at every intensity, indicating that adding more sites does not dilute the signal when within-site span is preserved. Random-5 is next best, drifting modestly downward as more sites are capped at five determinations; random-3 is lower and declines more steeply. Random-1 experiences the sharpest loss of power, falling from roughly three-quarters to less than half of replicates as the rule is applied to all sites.

The oldest and youngest strategies again perform poorly. The oldest rule collapses with intensity, moving from mid-range to low support. The youngest rule is low and essentially flat, consistent with its weak performance in accuracy. Bracketed is mid-tier and largely invariant with intensity: the additional information gained from using both boundaries helps discriminate growth families better than random-1 at higher sampling.

### 3.3 Synthesis

Taken together, the empirical (Fig. 2) and simulation (Figs 3–5) results clarify the mechanism visible in the AustArch reference dataset (Fig. 1). First, after normalisation, the choice among SPD, KDE, and FD smoothers is less important than what is dated within sites. Second, site coverage matters, but it cannot compensate for a poor within-site rule: forcing every site into oldest, youngest, or random-1 regimes reduces both accuracy and confidence, and induces large, directional parameter biases. Third, the efficient approach in resource-constrained settings is random-*n* with n ≥ 3, as this preserves within-site span, achieves high model recovery, yields negligible bias in slopes and rates, and maintains strong ΔAIC margins. The bracketed rule is an acceptable compromise when only two dates per site are feasible, and it does improve growth-model identification relative to random-1, but it introduces predictable bias that grows with intensity and should be acknowledged or corrected.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Within-site sampling as the dominant lever

Our study measured, under controlled conditions, how within-site sampling choices propagate into regional demographic inference from radiocarbon determinations. Once curves are normalised and placed on a common grid, the choice among SPDs, FDs, and KDEs is relatively minor compared with what gets dated within each site, and how many samples are collected. Rules that preserve within-site temporal span (using all dates or drawing a small random set of determinations per site) recover the correct growth-model family reliably, yield near-zero bias in growth parameters, and sustain clear ΔAIC margins. The oldest, youngest, and random-1 rules depress accuracy, induce strong directional bias, and erode evidential strength as their use becomes more pervasive. These outcomes arise even in an idealised archive where research intensity has been held constant, indicating that they are not generated by taphonomy.

The empirical AustArch panels show the mechanism without inviting historical interpretation. Starting from an all-dates baseline, imposing the oldest rule approximates a distribution of site onsets and shifts mass earlier; the youngest rule shifts mass later. The bracketed rule preserves span but reweights to edges of the curve, subtly lifting the early and very recent shoulders. The random-1 rule inflates between-realisation variance. Taking several random dates per site keeps the curves close to the baseline (i.e., closest to the full-data pattern). The simulations quantify these patterns: span-preserving strategies keep model-recovery accuracy high, parameter bias near zero, and evidential strength high, whereas oldest, youngest, and random-1 rules reduce performance, with the bracketed rule in between. Increasing site coverage cannot compensate for a biased within-site rule. It can instead increase precision around biased estimates.

### 4.2 Rethinking “dates-as-data” method debates

These results inform several long-running debates in the dates-as-data literature. Considerable attention has focused on the relative merits of SPDs, KDEs, and FD count aggregators, and on the extent to which calibration wiggles, bin widths, and bandwidths might mislead [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @brown2015FilterDarklyPopulation; @carleton2021SumThingsAre; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @crema2016SummedProbabilityDistribution; @riris2018DatesDataRevisited; @shennan2007PrehistoricPopulationHistory; @smith2016UseSummedprobabilityPlots; @surovell2007NoteUseTemporal; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. More recently, several authors have proposed fully model-based alternatives that treat radiocarbon determinations as events in count or Poisson processes, or as temporally uncertain observations in Bayesian frameworks, rather than as inputs to SPD [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @hanna2025SumPoissonApproach; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon]. Our findings identify a source of bias that precedes these analytical choices: if many sites contribute only their earliest or latest context, or a single arbitrary context, then the regional evidence is compromised before any SPD, KDE, or FD is drawn or any statistical model is fitted.

Uneven sampling has been flagged before [@contreras2014SummedRadiocarbonCalibrations; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon], and recent process-based models improve the treatment of regional counts and event rates [@crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @hanna2025SumPoissonApproach; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon], but the magnitude of within-site sampling effects and their interaction with model choice and parameter estimation have not, to our knowledge, been quantified under controlled conditions. Likelihood-based methods separate descriptive smoothing from demographic inference: SPDs, KDEs, and FDs become tools for exploring and communicating broad structure, while the demographic question is asked at the level of an explicit model fitted to calibrated posteriors. Studies comparing SPDs derived from different proxies, such as shoreline positions versus radiocarbon dates [@roalkvam2025ComparingSummedProbability], show that even methodologically careful curves can diverge sharply when the underlying sampling process differs. Our results show that within-site decisions on dating are a major component of that process.

In terms of population-growth modelling, this work contributes to the ongoing debate about using SPDs as input data for statistical models [@freeman2018CultureProcessInterpretation; @timpson2015InferentialMistakesPopulation; @torfing2015NeolithicPopulationSummed] and provides a robust model-selection approach. @timpson2021DirectlyModellingPopulation employed a marginal likelihood (our $\ell^1$) and used BIC to mitigate overfitting, which they attribute to deficiencies of alternative likelihood constructions. Here we use the OSE rule with AIC to mitigate overfitting [@yates2021ParsimoniousModelSelection], which retains an information-theoretic interpretation and identifies a parsimonious set of models with comparable expected predictive performance. Because our growth families are low-dimensional, we do not fully exploit the parsimony advantages of the OSE rule; for parameter-rich models (e.g., with multiple changepoints), it would be especially useful for selecting the simplest model whose $\Delta \mathrm{AIC}$ lies within one standard error of the minimum. Although we focus on the effects of sampling biases using a simple class of growth models, the likelihood framework is easily extended to more complex growth structures in both frequentist and Bayesian settings.

### 4.3 Implications for legacy datasets, study design and database reporting

Large, aggregated samples (for example, >50 dates) are often considered sufficient to reduce or remove local idiosyncrasies [see @timpson2014ReconstructingRegionalPopulation; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. Large global syntheses and regional validation exercises show why this assumption can be insufficient: large sample sizes can support demographic narratives or proxy tests [@brown2019MaoriPopulationGrowth; @chaput2016RadiocarbonDatesEstimates; @codding2024CountsRadiocarbondatedArchaeological; @porcic2016DemographyEarlyNeolithic; @wirtz2024MulticentennialCyclesContinental], yet they still inherit biases in the underlying dataset and sampling techniques. If within-site contributions are systematically oldest or youngest, adding more sites increases precision around a biased estimator. The temporal span covered within sites is therefore as important as the total number of determinations. In the simulations, random-*n* represents an unbiased benchmark rather than a literal field prescription. The practical implication is to date contexts that span the occupational sequence: the oldest secure context, the youngest secure context, and, where three or more dates are possible, one or more independent intermediate contexts rather than additional samples clustered at one end. A database with modest totals but consistent span-aware sampling can outperform a much larger database dominated by edge-only or one-date sites.

These problems are most acute for legacy datasets assembled during periods when radiocarbon determinations were expensive and laboratory capacity limited, and it was routine to date only one or two contexts per site. Contemporary projects, benefitting from cheaper AMS measurements and more explicit sampling designs, typically generate higher per-site counts and are less vulnerable to the worst-case scenarios we simulate here. Nonetheless, most large regional and continental compilations have large numbers of older, sparsely dated sites [@bird2022P3k14cSyntheticGlobal; @loftus2019ArchaeologicalRadiocarbonDatabase; @lucarini2020MedAfriCarbonRadiocarbonDatabase; @saktura2023SahulArchGeochronologicalDatabase], so the within-site biases we quantify are present in much of the evidence base. This does not mean all previous continental or regional models are invalid, nor that every site with fewer than three dates should be discarded mechanically. Regional analyses should instead include a sampling audit: report how many sites contribute one, two, three, or more determinations; test whether results change when one- and two-date sites are excluded, down-weighted, or analysed separately; and interpret patterns as strongest where they persist under those checks. Large datasets dominated by sites with three or more determinations are likely to be more robust, but intermixed sparse sites can still matter if they are concentrated in particular regions, periods, or site types.

The results have direct implications for field practices and sampling strategies. If budgets allow, dating all eligible contexts is preferred on every metric; when that is impossible, archaeologists should aim for at least three well-spaced determinations per site: the oldest and youngest secure contexts plus independent intermediate contexts. In our simulations, random-*n* approximates such unbiased coverage; in field practice, the goal is span-aware coverage rather than literal randomisation. Large-scale data-compilation and rescaling approaches now exist for constructing regional $^{14}$C datasets, standardising key metadata, and addressing inter-site sampling heterogeneity [e.g., @bird2022P3k14cSyntheticGlobal; @bunbury2025RobustDemographicModels; @loftus2019ArchaeologicalRadiocarbonDatabase; @lucarini2020MedAfriCarbonRadiocarbonDatabase; @park2026RescalingRadiocarbonData], but even careful workflows rarely encode the within-site selection rules that our simulations identify as a primary source of inferential risk.

Repositories should, wherever possible, record the within-site decisions that were made when choosing what to date, the date count for each site (that is, the number of radiocarbon data points it contributes), and simple descriptors of stratigraphic span. Analyses using those repositories should report their screening decisions, the distribution of date counts per site after screening, the model-selection scores or summary statistics used to support conclusions, and associated uncertainties, so that accuracy and confidence can be distinguished. Without such metadata it is difficult to know whether regional trends reflect human history, taphonomy, or the effects of oldest, youngest, or random-1 sampling; with it, analysts can run more realistic simulations, implement bias corrections for bracketed designs, and flag, down-weight, analyse separately, or exclude sites whose one- or two-date records make them especially vulnerable to within-site sampling bias.

### 4.4 Interactions with taphonomy and research intensity

Our experiments held taphonomy and research intensity constant to isolate the site-level mechanism, but in real archives both forces act together [@becerra-valdivia2020ChallengesSampleProcessing; @bluhm2019ValidationGlobalModel; @contreras2024LandscapeTaphonomyPredictably; @peros2010PrehistoricDemographyNorth; @shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @surovell2009CorrectingTemporalFrequency; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. In such settings, within-site selection can compound the elevation of recent samples and/or suppress genuine early peaks. Applications that read long-term robustness, hazard, or ecological interaction from radiocarbon-based SPDs and related summaries [@marom2024NotePredatorpreyDynamics; @roalkvam2025ComparingSummedProbability; @stutz2025HiddenHazardsLongTerm] inevitably reflect the combined effects of these processes, including the within-site rules we quantify here. The order of operations therefore matters: correcting for taphonomy on an evidence base already skewed by oldest, youngest, or random-1 practices risks compounding error.

A more robust workflow is to: i) review and, where possible, redesign site-level sampling to maximise well-spaced determinations; ii) fit population models to calibrated posteriors using a likelihood framework; and, if necessary, iii) layer taphonomic or research-intensity adjustments and test their leverage with simulations. This sequence keeps the largest controllable source of bias, within-site selection, closest to the data and exposes the incremental effect of taphonomic corrections against an archive that already preserves within-site span.

### 4.5 Limitations and future directions

The study is deliberately conservative in several respects. We consider three simple growth families on a fixed Holocene window, whereas real histories show pulses, plateaus, and regime shifts. The likelihood-based approach we use would extend naturally to piecewise-linear or spline-regularised families, but we have not fitted them here. Sites were treated as exchangeable, with spatial heterogeneity and material-specific offsets not modelled explicitly. Calibration-curve uncertainty and laboratory systematics were propagated through the posteriors but were not treated as additional sources of parameter uncertainty, and we did not attempt to model the process by which contexts are selected for dating.

The Holocene focus does not imply that the problem is Holocene-specific. Pleistocene applications are likely to face the same within-site selection issue, compounded by stronger taphonomic loss, fewer well-dated sites, wider calibration uncertainty, and larger material or reservoir-offset effects. Extending the framework to Pleistocene windows is therefore a priority, but would require period-specific treatment of preservation, calibration uncertainty, and sparse-site structure.

Our findings identify several opportunities for further analysis, including: i) embedding the date-level likelihoods in hierarchical models that pool information across regions and materials while allowing local deviation; ii) designing bias-aware estimators for bracketed sampling, providing corrected parameters with appropriate uncertainty; and iii) building “design-to-inference” workflows that allow for translation of budget scenarios into expected accuracy, bias, and statistical power. Existing probabilistic and hierarchical treatments of radiocarbon and related temporal data [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @crema2017SpatiotemporalApproachesArchaeological; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @demjan2016ModellingDistributionArchaeological; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon] provide ready templates for these extensions that could be coupled explicitly to models of within-site selection. More generally, the framework could be adapted to richer demographic models, including pulse-like events and multi-phase histories, while retaining the core approach of modelling each calibrated determination as a probability distribution rather than a single point.

### 4.6 Conclusions

Overall, our analysis provides three key findings for the use of radiocarbon data as a proxy for past demographic and environmental change. First, SPDs, KDEs, and FDs remain valuable as descriptive summaries and for broader communication, but they should not bear the full weight of inference. Likelihood-based methods allow demographic questions to be posed at the level of explicit models while preserving the calibration and uncertainty of each determination. Second, sampling strategies for archaeologists should seek to recover a minimum of three chronological determinations that maximise the temporal span within the site, rather than relying on random-1 sampling or oldest or youngest strata alone. Although three or five dates per site may be expensive, a modest number of well-chosen determinations per site can reduce the risk of regional programmes producing precise but biased answers. Third, regional syntheses should not simply pool all available sites and assume large totals solve the problem. They should audit per-site date counts and run sensitivity analyses that exclude, down-weight, or separately report sites with fewer than three determinations, especially in legacy datasets.

In summary, “dates-as-data” demography depends on the within-site decisions that form the foundation of the datasets used. Our empirical demonstrations show how strongly aggregate curves respond to those decisions; our simulations quantify the resulting losses in model recovery, parameter accuracy, and evidential strength; and our likelihood-based framework provides a way forward that is not dependent on smoothing choices.
