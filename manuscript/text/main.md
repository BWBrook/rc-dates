# Within-site sampling influences radiocarbon “dates-as-data” demographic reconstructions

<div id="authors"></div>

_Word count:_ ~3,500 (Abstract to Discussion)

## Abstract

Radiocarbon "dates-as-data" is widely used to investigate past demographic and environmental change, but the effect of within-site sampling decisions remains poorly quantified. We combine a Holocene subset of the AustArch database with simulations to test how common site-level sampling rules affect summed probability distributions (SPDs), kernel density estimates (KDEs), frequency distributions (FDs), and likelihood-based demographic inference. Empirical experiments compared five down-sampling rules against an all-dates baseline: random-1, random-5, oldest, youngest, and bracketed (oldest + youngest). Simulations added random-3 and evaluated 50%, 75%, and 100% site-level application. Simulated radiocarbon determinations were generated under uniform, linear, and exponential growth models, with site counts, per-site sample sizes, and age-error structure parameterised from AustArch. Inference used likelihoods fitted directly to calibrated posteriors, with model choice by Akaike's Information Criterion. All-dates and random-*n* strategies using three or more dates consistently produced high model recovery, negligible parameter bias, and strong model-selection support. Oldest, youngest, and random-1 rules reduced accuracy, induced directional bias, and weakened support for the preferred model. Once curves were normalised and aligned, differences among SPD, KDE, and FD summaries were secondary to within-site sampling. We conclude that radiocarbon modelling should prioritise temporal span within sites. When exhaustive dating is not possible, the simulation results support a practical minimum of three determinations per site, translated in field settings into well-spaced samples that span the occupational sequence.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 “Dates as data” for reconstructing past human population change

Archaeologists increasingly use radiocarbon determinations as data for population inference, aggregating them to track temporal change in human activity and, by proxy, population size [@rick1987DatesDataExamination; @robinson2019SpatiotemporalDynamicsPrehistoric; @ulm1996RadiocarbonCulturalChronologies; @williams2016RadiometricDatesAre]. The rationale is that, all else being equal, intervals with more dated activity represent intervals with more people doing datable things. This logic has been applied to European Neolithic demography [@shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @timpson2014ReconstructingRegionalPopulation], Holocene Sahul [@johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient; @williams2013NewPopulationCurve; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon], other regional sequences [@brown2019MaoriPopulationGrowth; @chaput2016RadiocarbonDatesEstimates; @porcic2016DemographyEarlyNeolithic], and continental syntheses [e.g., @wirtz2024MulticentennialCyclesContinental]. Several summaries and models are in common use, including summed probability distributions (SPDs), frequency distributions (FDs), kernel density estimates (KDEs), and explicit population or occupancy models [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @brown2015FilterDarklyPopulation; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient; @mendez-quiros2023ModelingLongtermHuman; @michczynska2007FrequencyDistributionRadiocarbon; @robinson2019SpatiotemporalDynamicsPrehistoric; @surovell2007NoteUseTemporal; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon].

Known biases complicate these approaches. Physical processes cause differential preservation of older archaeological material, producing the familiar “pull of the recent” [@bluhm2019ValidationGlobalModel; @surovell2009CorrectingTemporalFrequency]. Research effort varies across space and time, and recent rescaling work shows that inter-settlement sampling intensity can affect SPD-based reconstructions [@park2026RescalingRadiocarbonData]. Calibration, binning, smoothing, and model choice can also shape inferred trajectories [@carleton2021SumThingsAre; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @ramsey2024DevelopmentINTCALDatabase]. These issues have motivated taphonomic adjustments, binning protocols, simulation tests, and alternatives that treat dated events as count or Poisson processes rather than as smoothed probability densities [@crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @hanna2025SumPoissonApproach; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon; @peros2010PrehistoricDemographyNorth; @shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon; @williams2015HoloceneDemographicChanges]. A further source of distortion sits closer to dataset formation: which contexts within each site are dated, and how many samples each site contributes [@williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. This study examines that within-site source of distortion.

### 1.2 Within-site sampling as a dataset-formation problem

Archaeologists rarely date every context at a site. Budgets, research aims, sample availability, preservation constraints, and development-led cultural resource management can favour targeted minimal dating over exhaustive chronology [@codding2024CountsRadiocarbondatedArchaeological]. Common choices include dating the oldest layer to anchor a sequence, the youngest to bracket occupation, one representative hearth, or the oldest and youngest contexts together [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @price2021EndtoendBayesianAnalysis]. Radiocarbon databases therefore contain many single-date and two-date sites, especially in legacy compilations assembled when dating was more expensive and less routine [@saktura2023SahulArchGeochronologicalDatabase; @williams2014AustArchDatabase14C; @williams2013NewPopulationCurve].

Such choices can skew regional aggregates before any smoothing or statistical model is applied. If many sites contribute only their oldest or youngest context, regional curves can shift earlier or later; if one-date sites dominate, within-site temporal variance is suppressed. Because these decisions are heterogeneous and often undocumented, their effects are difficult to separate from taphonomy, research intensity, or calibration structure in observational datasets.

We address this gap by combining a Holocene reference set from the AustArch radiocarbon database of Sahul [@williams2014AustArchDatabase14C] with controlled simulations. AustArch provides realistic site-level date counts, temporal spans, and age-error structure. We first show how common within-site rules reshape aggregate SPDs, KDEs, and FDs, and then simulate datasets with known population trajectories to test how those rules affect model recovery, parameter bias, and evidential strength.

Our inferential approach keeps the uncertainty attached to each radiocarbon determination. Rather than smoothing all dates into a regional curve and treating that curve as population history, we ask which simple population-growth model would make the calibrated dates most likely. Model performance is summarised with maximum likelihood and Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) [@akaike1974NewLookStatistical], while SPDs, KDEs, and FDs are retained as descriptive plots. The paper is methodological: it does not reconstruct Australian population history or correct for taphonomy or research intensity. Instead, it measures how within-site selection changes the evidence base that those broader analyses depend on, complementing recent probabilistic frameworks for radiocarbon-driven palaeodemography [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon].

## 2. Methods

Full filtering, calibration, likelihood, and simulation details for reproducibility are provided in the Supplementary Methods; the code and data package will be supplied through a de-identified private-for-review repository record with a reserved DOI before submission. Here we summarise the design choices needed to interpret the results.

### 2.1 Empirical reference set and descriptive curves

We used AustArch, a continental radiocarbon compilation for Sahul [@williams2014AustArchDatabase14C], to build a realistic Holocene reference set. Records were screened to retain radiocarbon determinations from 500-20,000 $^{14}$C yr BP with reported ages and one-sigma errors, remove records flagged as erroneous or unrelated to human activity, and restrict materials and contexts to those suitable for archaeological demographic comparison. Dates were calibrated with SHCal20 or Marine20; marine shell determinations used Marine20 without site-specific $\Delta R$ corrections because harmonised offsets were unavailable across the database. Calibrated records were retained when their posterior grids overlapped the 11,650-651 cal BP analysis window, and summaries and likelihoods were evaluated within that window. We retained sites with five or more valid Holocene determinations, yielding 976 determinations from 116 sites. This deliberately well-dated reference set is a benchmarking substrate rather than an unbiased demographic sample of Sahul: it allows us to measure how much chronological information is removed when sparse-site sampling rules are imposed.

For descriptive comparison, we constructed three common aggregate summaries on a shared 100-year calendar grid: summed probability distributions (SPDs), frequency distributions (FDs), and kernel density estimates (KDEs). Each curve was normalised to unit area. These summaries provide visual context only; the model-fitting analyses used the calibrated posteriors directly.

### 2.2 Empirical within-site sub-sampling experiments

We imposed five within-site rules on the AustArch reference set: random-1, random-5, oldest, youngest, and bracketed (oldest + youngest). Each rule was applied to either 50% or 100% of sites, with untreated sites contributing all dates; the all-dates baseline is therefore the 0% case. Stochastic rules used 1,000 Monte Carlo realisations per scenario, and deterministic rules used 1,000 site-subset draws where applicable. For each realisation we selected dates under the rule, recalibrated where required, recomputed SPD, FD, and KDE summaries, and compared the resulting curves with the all-dates baseline. These experiments were diagnostic: they show how site-level sampling rules deform regional aggregates, not how AustArch itself should be read as a demographic history.

### 2.3 Simulation study and model fitting

The simulation study used the filtered AustArch data to parameterise realistic site structures. Per-site sample sizes were drawn from a truncated negative-binomial model fitted to the empirical date-count distribution, and measurement uncertainty was modelled from the observed relationship between radiocarbon age and logged standard error. Synthetic datasets were generated over the Holocene window from three unit-normalised growth models: uniform, linearly increasing, and exponentially increasing. For the linear and exponential models, the simulated distribution corresponded to a 10-fold population increase, close to previous estimates for Holocene Sahul [@johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient].

Each simulated dataset was then subjected to seven sampling strategies: all dates, random-1, random-3, random-5, oldest, youngest, and bracketed. The sub-sampling strategies were applied to 50%, 75%, or 100% of sites, with unaffected sites contributing all dates. We generated 1,000 datasets for each population model and fitted all three candidate models to every sampled dataset.

Inference was based on maximum-likelihood fitting to calibrated posteriors rather than to smoothed aggregate curves. We used Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) [@akaike1974NewLookStatistical] and a modified one-standard-error rule [@yates2021ParsimoniousModelSelection] to identify models with comparable support while accounting for estimation uncertainty. Performance was summarised by: i) accuracy, the proportion of simulations selecting the true growth family; ii) parameter bias for the linear and exponential models; and iii) evidential strength, the proportion of simulations where the one-standard-error rule selected only the true model. Bootstrap resampling was used to generate confidence intervals for accuracy estimates.

### 2.4 Reproducibility

All analyses were run in R using rcarbon for calibration and standard tidyverse workflows for data handling [@crema2021InferenceLargeSets; @rcoreteam2025LanguageEnvironmentStatistical]. The review archive contains the input data, analysis code, tests, `{targets}` workflow, and pinned `renv` environment needed to inspect and rerun the code-level analysis package outside GitHub. The final repository record will include the locked review archive, private review link, and reserved DOI before submission, and will be made public after acceptance.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Empirical behaviour of the AustArch reference set

Figure 1 shows the 0% sub-sampling baseline: all eligible determinations calibrated with SHCal20 or Marine20, then summarised as SPD, KDE, and FD curves on a common 100-year grid. At centennial resolution, the three aggregators agree closely. The FD is jagged by construction, the KDE is slightly smoother, and the smoothed SPD tracks the KDE. The curves show low and noisy early-mid Holocene values, a gradual rise through the mid-to-late Holocene, and a steep increase in the last two millennia. Since each curve is normalised to unit area, these displays describe relative density only and serve as a visual reference for the sub-sampling experiments.

Figure 2 illustrates how plausible within-site sampling rules deform this baseline when applied to 50% or 100% of sites. Choosing the oldest determination per site converts the dataset toward a distribution of site onsets. At 100% intensity the FD develops step-like mid-Holocene plateaus and the SPD/KDE shift mass from the most recent centuries into earlier periods; at 50% intensity these effects are diluted by the untreated half of sites. Selecting the youngest determination produces the mirror distortion. With 100% intensity the very recent peak is attenuated, and structure is redistributed into the mid-to-late Holocene; at 50% the curves track the baseline more closely but still show a damped recent amplitude.

The bracketed rule (oldest + youngest) preserves site spans but reweights probability toward their edges. Even when applied to every site, the overall shape remains similar to the baseline, yet the very late Holocene mass is only modestly elevated relative to mid-span levels. The random-1 rule (one random determination per treated site) is approximately unbiased in expectation but volatile: Monte Carlo ribbons widen across the Holocene, especially with 100% sampling intensity when every site is reduced to a single date. The random-5 rule preserves span while capping per-site influence; the resulting curves remain close to the baseline and uncertainty ribbons are narrow. Across all panels, differences among SPD, KDE, and FD are minor compared with the effect of the within-site rule. These results motivate the simulation analysis.

### 3.2 Simulation experiments

All simulation results reported here use the $\ell^1$ likelihood; the $\ell^2$ variant yields qualitatively identical patterns (Supplementary Information). Figures 3–5 report model recovery, parameter bias, and evidential strength for each within-site sampling rule and intensity; the all-dates reference has no sub-sampling intensity and is repeated across the intensity axis for comparison.

#### 3.2.1 Model recovery

Model recovery is summarised in Figure 3. Span-preserving strategies perform strongly and are insensitive to intensity. The repeated all-dates reference and random-5 recover the correct family in roughly 0.92–0.96 of replicates across 50%, 75%, and 100% application. Random-3 performs slightly worse but remains high and stable. Reducing each treated site to one random date (random-1) leads to a monotonic decline: performance near 0.95 when only half of sites are affected falls into the mid-0.8s when every site is reduced to one date.

The oldest and youngest rules fare poorly. The oldest rule appears accurate at low intensity but collapses as the rule is applied to more sites, falling to roughly one-third correct at 100%. The youngest rule performs poorly at all intensities, with accuracy around 0.4 and even worse performance as more sites are subjected to the rule. The bracketed rule is mid-tier (~0.65) and largely flat across intensities: it clearly outperforms either oldest or youngest alone, but it does not match the span-preserving random-sampling strategies.

#### 3.2.2 Parameter bias

Parameter estimation gives a sharper view of these differences (Fig. 4). When the generating model is exponential, the growth rate is effectively unbiased under all dates and random-*n*. The oldest and youngest rules show strong, sign-opposed biases that increase with intensity: oldest underestimates the rate (negative bias), youngest overestimates it (positive bias), and bracketed inherits a milder averaged negative bias. Under linear truth, slopes behave similarly on a tighter scale.

#### 3.2.3 Evidential strength

The evidential-strength results distinguish high model recovery from unambiguous model support (Fig. 5). The repeated all-dates reference is near 0.9, indicating that the signal is strong when within-site span is fully preserved. Random-5 is next best, drifting modestly downward as more sites are capped at five determinations; random-3 is lower and declines more steeply. Random-1 experiences the sharpest loss of power, falling from roughly three-quarters to less than half of replicates as the rule is applied to all sites.

The oldest and youngest strategies again perform poorly. The oldest rule collapses with intensity, moving from mid-range to low support. The youngest rule is low and essentially flat, consistent with its weak performance in accuracy. Bracketed is mid-tier and largely invariant with intensity: the additional information gained from using both boundaries helps discriminate growth families better than random-1 at higher sampling.

### 3.3 Synthesis

Taken together, the empirical (Fig. 2) and simulation (Figs 3–5) results clarify the mechanism visible in the AustArch reference dataset (Fig. 1). First, after normalisation, the choice among SPD, KDE, and FD smoothers is less important than what is dated within sites. Second, site coverage matters, but it cannot compensate for a poor within-site rule: forcing every site into oldest, youngest, or random-1 regimes reduces both accuracy and confidence, and induces large, directional parameter biases. Third, the efficient approach in resource-constrained settings is random-*n* using three or more dates, as this preserves within-site span, achieves high model recovery, yields negligible bias in slopes and rates, and maintains strong ΔAIC margins. The bracketed rule is an acceptable compromise when only two dates per site are feasible, and it does improve growth-model identification relative to random-1, but it introduces predictable bias that grows with intensity and should be acknowledged or corrected.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Within-site sampling as the dominant lever

Our study measured how within-site sampling choices propagate into regional demographic inference from radiocarbon determinations. Once curves are normalised and placed on a common grid, the choice among SPDs, FDs, and KDEs is smaller than the effect of what gets dated within sites, and how many samples are collected. Rules that preserve within-site temporal span recover the correct growth-model family reliably, yield near-zero bias in growth parameters, and sustain clear ΔAIC margins. Oldest, youngest, and random-1 rules depress accuracy, induce directional bias, and erode evidential strength. These outcomes arise in an idealised archive where research intensity is held constant, indicating that they are not generated by taphonomy.

The AustArch panels show the mechanism without requiring historical interpretation. The oldest rule shifts mass earlier by approximating a distribution of site onsets; the youngest rule shifts mass later; the bracketed rule preserves span but reweights the curve toward its edges; and random-1 inflates between-realisation variance. The simulations quantify the same pattern. Span-preserving strategies maintain model recovery, low bias, and strong support, whereas oldest, youngest, and random-1 rules reduce performance, with the bracketed rule intermediate. Increasing site coverage cannot compensate for a biased within-site rule. It can instead make biased estimates look more precise.

### 4.2 Implications for dates-as-data methods

These results sharpen, rather than replace, existing debates about SPDs, KDEs, FDs, and model-based approaches [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @brown2015FilterDarklyPopulation; @carleton2021SumThingsAre; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @crema2016SummedProbabilityDistribution; @riris2018DatesDataRevisited; @shennan2007PrehistoricPopulationHistory; @smith2016UseSummedprobabilityPlots; @surovell2007NoteUseTemporal; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. Recent count-based and Bayesian frameworks improve how radiocarbon events are modelled once data are assembled [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @hanna2025SumPoissonApproach; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon]. Our results identify a prior source of bias: if many sites contribute only their earliest context, latest context, or a single arbitrary context, the regional evidence is compromised before any curve is drawn or model is fitted.

The likelihood framework used here separates descriptive smoothing from demographic inference. SPDs, KDEs, and FDs remain useful for exploration and communication, but the demographic question is asked at the level of explicit growth models fitted to calibrated posteriors. This complements work showing that radiocarbon curves can diverge from other proxies when the underlying sampling process differs [@roalkvam2025ComparingSummedProbability]. It also extends population-growth modelling debates around SPD inputs and likelihood construction [@freeman2018CultureProcessInterpretation; @timpson2015InferentialMistakesPopulation; @timpson2021DirectlyModellingPopulation; @torfing2015NeolithicPopulationSummed]. We used AIC with a one-standard-error rule to avoid over-interpreting small differences among models [@yates2021ParsimoniousModelSelection]; the same framework could be extended to richer growth families, including multi-phase or changepoint models.

### 4.3 Implications for legacy datasets, study design and repositories

Large aggregated samples are often treated as sufficient to dilute local idiosyncrasies [see @timpson2014ReconstructingRegionalPopulation; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon], but this is unsafe when the same within-site bias is repeated across many sites. If site contributions are systematically oldest or youngest, adding more sites can make the biased result look more precise. Temporal span within sites is therefore as important as the total number of determinations. Smaller datasets with well-spaced site sequences may be more informative than larger datasets dominated by edge-only or one-date records. In the simulations, random-*n* is an unbiased benchmark rather than a literal field prescription. In practice, the aim is to date contexts that span the occupational sequence: the oldest secure context, the youngest secure context, and, where three or more dates are possible, independent intermediate contexts.

The risk is greatest in legacy datasets assembled when dating was expensive and one- or two-date sites were common. This does not make previous regional or continental models invalid, nor does it imply that every site with fewer than three dates should be discarded. It does mean that regional analyses should audit site-level date counts and test whether results change when one- and two-date sites are excluded, down-weighted, or analysed separately. Large datasets dominated by sites with three or more determinations are likely to be more robust, but sparse sites can still matter if they cluster in particular regions, periods, materials, or site types [@bird2022P3k14cSyntheticGlobal; @loftus2019ArchaeologicalRadiocarbonDatabase; @lucarini2020MedAfriCarbonRadiocarbonDatabase; @saktura2023SahulArchGeochronologicalDatabase].

For new work, the practical guidance is simple. If budgets allow, date all eligible contexts. If not, the simulations support three determinations as a practical minimum, and field sampling should make those dates deliberately well spaced: oldest and youngest secure contexts plus one or more independent intermediate samples. For databases, record site-level date counts (the number of radiocarbon determinations each site contributes), stratigraphic span, and, where known, the selection logic behind dated contexts. Recent large-scale data-compilation and rescaling approaches improve inter-site standardisation and sampling balance [@bird2022P3k14cSyntheticGlobal; @bunbury2025RobustDemographicModels; @loftus2019ArchaeologicalRadiocarbonDatabase; @lucarini2020MedAfriCarbonRadiocarbonDatabase; @park2026RescalingRadiocarbonData], but within-site selection rules also need to be visible if analysts are to distinguish accuracy from confidence.

### 4.4 Limitations and future directions

Our experiments held taphonomy and research intensity constant to isolate within-site selection, but real archives combine all three processes [@becerra-valdivia2020ChallengesSampleProcessing; @bluhm2019ValidationGlobalModel; @contreras2024LandscapeTaphonomyPredictably; @peros2010PrehistoricDemographyNorth; @shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @surovell2009CorrectingTemporalFrequency; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. Within-site selection can therefore compound the pull of recent samples or suppress genuine early peaks, and taphonomic correction applied to a biased evidence base may compound error. A robust workflow should first maximise or audit within-site span, then fit population models to calibrated posteriors, and then test the additional leverage of taphonomic or research-intensity adjustments.

The study is deliberately conservative. We consider three simple growth families over a fixed Holocene window, treat sites as exchangeable, and do not model spatial heterogeneity, material-specific offsets, or the process by which contexts are selected for dating. Marine shell determinations were calibrated with Marine20 without site-specific $\Delta R$ corrections because harmonised offsets were not available across the database; results are therefore interpreted as sampling-design experiments rather than demographic reconstructions. The problem is not Holocene-specific: Pleistocene applications face the same within-site issue, compounded by stronger taphonomic loss, fewer well-dated sites, wider calibration uncertainty, and larger material or reservoir-offset effects. Future work should extend the framework to hierarchical regional models, richer demographic histories, and bias-aware estimators for bracketed sampling, while retaining the core principle of modelling calibrated determinations rather than treating smoothed curves as the inferential object [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @crema2017SpatiotemporalApproachesArchaeological; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @demjan2016ModellingDistributionArchaeological; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon].

### 4.5 Conclusions

Overall, our analysis provides three practical conclusions for radiocarbon-based demographic and environmental inference. First, SPDs, KDEs, and FDs remain valuable descriptive summaries, but they should not carry the full inferential burden. Second, field sampling should prioritise within-site temporal span; where exhaustive dating is impossible, the simulations support at least three determinations as a practical minimum, translated into well-spaced samples where possible. Third, regional syntheses should audit per-site date counts and test sensitivity to sparse sites, especially in legacy datasets. “Dates-as-data” demography depends not only on how radiocarbon dates are modelled, but also on how the dated contexts entered the dataset in the first place.
