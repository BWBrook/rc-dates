# Within-site sampling influences radiocarbon “dates-as-data” demographic reconstructions

_Word count:_ ~3,800 (Abstract to Discussion); ~4,960 including figure legends

## Abstract

Radiocarbon "dates-as-data" is widely used to investigate past demographic and environmental change, but the effect of within-site sampling decisions remains poorly quantified. We combine a Holocene subset of the AustArch database with simulations to test how site-level sampling rules affect summed probability distributions (SPDs), kernel density estimates (KDEs), frequency distributions (FDs), and likelihood-based demographic inference. Empirical experiments compared five down-sampling rules against an all-dates baseline: random-1, random-5, oldest, youngest, and bracketed (oldest + youngest). Simulations added random-3 and evaluated 50%, 75%, and 100% site-level application. Synthetic dates were independent draws from uniform, linear, or exponential regional trajectories, with site counts, per-site sample sizes, and reported-error structure parameterised from AustArch. Under this idealised design, all-dates and random samples of three or five dates produced high model recovery and negligible parameter bias. A paired realised-assay sensitivity preserved this ranking among all-dates and random rules, with cellwise accuracy and support shifts below two percentage points. Oldest and youngest sampling induced directional bias and, with random-1, reduced accuracy or support. In the normalised empirical displays, deformation caused by within-site sampling exceeded the visual differences among SPD, KDE, and FD summaries. The results show that site-level selection rules require explicit audit and sensitivity analysis. Distributing multiple determinations across secure occupational contexts remains a sensible field heuristic, but these simulations neither reproduce site occupation histories nor identify a universal three-date minimum.

radiocarbon dating; archaeological demography; within-site sampling; dates-as-data; summed probability distributions; likelihood-based inference; simulation.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 “Dates as data” for reconstructing past human population change

Archaeologists increasingly use radiocarbon determinations as data for population inference, aggregating them to track temporal change in human activity and, by proxy, population size [@rick1987DatesDataExamination; @robinson2019SpatiotemporalDynamicsPrehistoric; @ulm1996RadiocarbonCulturalChronologies; @williams2016RadiometricDatesAre]. The rationale is that, all else being equal, intervals with more dated activity represent intervals with more people doing datable things. This logic has been applied to European Neolithic demography [@shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @timpson2014ReconstructingRegionalPopulation], Holocene Sahul [@johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient; @williams2013NewPopulationCurve; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon], other regional sequences [@brown2019MaoriPopulationGrowth; @chaput2016RadiocarbonDatesEstimates; @porcic2016DemographyEarlyNeolithic], and continental syntheses [e.g., @wirtz2024MulticentennialCyclesContinental]. Several summaries and models are in common use, including summed probability distributions (SPDs), frequency distributions (FDs), kernel density estimates (KDEs), and explicit population or occupancy models [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @brown2015FilterDarklyPopulation; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient; @mendez-quiros2023ModelingLongtermHuman; @michczynska2007FrequencyDistributionRadiocarbon; @robinson2019SpatiotemporalDynamicsPrehistoric; @surovell2007NoteUseTemporal; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon].

Known biases complicate these approaches. Physical processes cause differential preservation of older archaeological material, producing the familiar “pull of the recent” [@bluhm2019ValidationGlobalModel; @surovell2009CorrectingTemporalFrequency]. Research effort varies across space and time, and recent rescaling work shows that inter-settlement sampling intensity can affect SPD-based reconstructions [@park2026RescalingRadiocarbonData]. Calibration, binning, smoothing, and model choice can also alter inferred trajectories [@carleton2021SumThingsAre; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric]. These issues have motivated taphonomic adjustments, binning protocols, simulation tests, and alternatives that treat dated events as count or Poisson processes rather than as smoothed probability densities [@crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @hanna2025SumPoissonApproach; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon; @peros2010PrehistoricDemographyNorth; @shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon; @williams2015HoloceneDemographicChanges]. A further source of distortion sits closer to dataset formation: which contexts within each site are dated, and how many samples each site contributes [@williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. This study examines that within-site source of distortion.

### 1.2 Within-site sampling as a dataset-formation problem

Archaeologists rarely date every context at a site. Budgets, research aims, sample availability, preservation constraints, and development-led cultural resource management can favour targeted minimal dating over exhaustive chronology [@codding2024CountsRadiocarbondatedArchaeological]. A programme may date a basal context to constrain occupation onset, a terminal context to constrain its end, one accessible feature, or both ends of a sequence. Radiocarbon databases consequently contain many sparsely dated sites, especially in legacy compilations assembled when dating was more expensive and less routine [@saktura2023SahulArchGeochronologicalDatabase; @williams2013NewPopulationCurve]. In the original AustArch compilation, 53% of sites had one radiocarbon determination and 86% had fewer than four [@williams2014AustArchDatabase14C].

Such choices can skew regional aggregates before any smoothing or statistical model is applied. If many sites contribute only their oldest or youngest context, regional curves can shift earlier or later; if one-date sites dominate, within-site temporal variance is suppressed. Because these decisions are heterogeneous and often undocumented, their effects are difficult to separate from taphonomy, research intensity, or calibration structure in observational datasets.

To isolate this effect, we combined a Holocene reference set from the AustArch radiocarbon database of Sahul [@williams2014AustArchDatabase14C] with controlled simulations. The empirical reference set supplies observed site-level date counts, temporal spans, and age-error structure. The simulations use its site count, date-count distribution, and age-error relationship, but not the observed site spans. We first tested how common within-site rules reshape aggregate SPDs, KDEs, and FDs. We then simulated datasets with known population trajectories to measure their effects on model recovery and parameter bias.

Our inferential approach keeps the uncertainty attached to each radiocarbon determination. Rather than smoothing all dates into a regional curve and treating that curve as population history, we ask which simple population-growth model would make the calibrated dates most likely. Model performance is summarised with maximum likelihood and Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) [@akaike1974NewLookStatistical], while SPDs, KDEs, and FDs are retained as descriptive plots. The paper is methodological: it does not reconstruct Australian population history or correct for taphonomy or research intensity. Instead, it measures how within-site selection changes the evidence base that those broader analyses depend on, complementing recent probabilistic frameworks for radiocarbon-driven palaeodemography [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon].

## 2. Methods

Full filtering, calibration, likelihood, and simulation details are given in the Supplementary Methods. A reviewer-facing repository record will be locked after the revised outputs and data-sharing conditions have been checked. Here we summarise the design choices needed to interpret the results.

### 2.1 Empirical reference set and descriptive curves

We used AustArch, a continental radiocarbon compilation for Sahul [@williams2014AustArchDatabase14C], to build a realistic Holocene reference set. Records were screened to retain radiocarbon determinations from 500-20,000 ¹⁴C yr BP with reported ages and one-sigma errors, remove records flagged as erroneous or unrelated to human activity, and restrict materials and contexts to those suitable for archaeological demographic comparison. Dates were calibrated with SHCal20 or Marine20 according to each record's marine-context classification; determinations assigned Marine20 received no site-specific $\Delta R$ correction because harmonised offsets were unavailable across the database. Calibrated records were retained when their posterior grids overlapped the 11,650-651 cal BP analysis window, and summaries and likelihoods were evaluated within that window. We retained sites with five or more valid Holocene determinations, yielding 976 determinations from 116 sites. This deliberately well-dated set is a benchmark, not an unbiased demographic sample of Sahul: it allows us to measure how much chronological information is removed when sparse-site sampling rules are imposed.

For descriptive comparison, we constructed three aggregate summaries on a shared 100-year calendar grid: summed probability distributions (SPDs), frequency distributions (FDs), and kernel density estimates (KDEs). The SPD used posterior mass from all 976 overlap-eligible dates; FD and KDE construction used the 889 dates whose weighted-mean calibrated ages also lay inside the analysis window. Each curve was normalised to unit area, and the FD received the same two-bin display smoothing in Figures 1 and 2. These method-specific summaries provide visual context only; the model-fitting analyses used the calibrated posteriors directly.

### 2.2 Empirical within-site sub-sampling experiments

We imposed five within-site rules on the AustArch reference set: random-1, random-5, oldest, youngest, and bracketed (oldest + youngest). Each rule was applied to either 50% or 100% of sites, with untreated sites contributing all dates; the all-dates baseline is therefore the 0% case. Stochastic rules used 1,000 Monte Carlo realisations per scenario, and deterministic rules used 1,000 site-subset draws where applicable. Empirical oldest and youngest rules used conventional radiocarbon ages. Calibration grids were computed once, then subset under each rule before SPD, FD, and KDE curves were recomputed and normalised. Pointwise 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles describe variation across the conditional realisations, not sampling confidence for the underlying archive. These experiments were diagnostic: they show how site-level sampling rules deform regional aggregates, not how AustArch itself should be read as a demographic history.

### 2.3 Simulation study and model fitting

The simulation study used the filtered AustArch data to parameterise 116 sites, per-site sample sizes, and the relationship between radiocarbon age and logged standard error. Conditional on each regional trajectory, calendar ages were drawn independently for all determinations; site identity set the number of dates and subsequent selection rule, but not a site-specific occupation window or within-site temporal dependence. Synthetic conventional ages in the production experiment were placed at the SHCal20 curve expectation and assigned empirically modelled one-sigma errors before recalibration. A focused sensitivity independently reran paired curve-expectation and realised conventional-age arms for all-dates, random-1, random-3, and random-5 (1,000 pairs per model-intensity cell), preserving latent data and selections within each pair rather than reusing the locked production rows. The study therefore tests selection among exchangeable regional draws under an idealised observation process rather than reproducing site occupation histories. Dates were generated over the Holocene window from three unit-normalised trajectories: constant, linearly increasing, and exponentially increasing towards the present. The linear and exponential trajectories represented a 10-fold increase, chosen as a strong Holocene-scale growth signal consistent with the qualitative increase inferred for Sahul by @johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient.

Each simulated dataset was then subjected to seven sampling strategies: all dates, random-1, random-3, random-5, oldest, youngest, and bracketed. Simulated oldest and youngest rules used median calibrated ages. The sub-sampling strategies were applied to 50%, 75%, or 100% of sites, with unaffected sites contributing all dates. We generated 1,000 datasets for each population model and fitted all three candidate models to every sampled dataset.

Inference was based on maximum-likelihood fitting to calibrated posteriors rather than to smoothed aggregate curves. We used Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC) [@akaike1974NewLookStatistical] to select among the three candidate growth families. We also applied a one-standard-error-style parsimony rule [cf. @yates2021ParsimoniousModelSelection]: models were treated as similarly supported when their total $\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$ did not exceed the estimated standard error of that pointwise AIC difference, after which only models of the lowest parameter dimension were retained. Performance was summarised by model-recovery accuracy, bias in the fitted linear or exponential growth parameter, and unambiguous support--the proportion of replicates in which the parsimony rule retained only the generating family. Accuracy and unambiguous-support intervals used 1,000 paired cluster-bootstrap resamples of replicate identifiers, with all three generating-family outcomes retained together in each resampled cluster.

### 2.4 Reproducibility

All analyses were run in R using rcarbon for calibration and standard tidyverse workflows for data handling [@crema2021InferenceLargeSets; @rcoreteam2025LanguageEnvironmentStatistical]. The analysis repository pins R 4.5.3 and its package environment, tests the consequential scientific transformations, and binds accepted high-volume outputs to checksums and frozen generation sources. A reviewer-facing archive will be assembled after the revised outputs are frozen; inclusion and licensing of source and filtered data will be confirmed before deposit. The final record will include a private review link and reserved DOI before submission and will be made public after acceptance, subject to those data-sharing conditions.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Empirical behaviour of the AustArch reference set

Figure 1 shows the 0% sub-sampling baseline on a common 100-year grid. The method-specific curves have broadly similar shapes: low and variable early-to-mid Holocene density, a gradual mid-to-late Holocene rise, and a steep increase in the last two millennia. The two-bin-smoothed FD remains more angular than the KDE, while the smoothed SPD tracks both. Because each curve is normalised separately, these displays describe relative shape rather than date count or population abundance.

Figure 2 shows how the within-site rules deform this baseline when applied to 50% or 100% of sites. Choosing the oldest determination shifts mass away from the recent rise and, at 100% application, produces a broad distribution whose largest feature lies near 8,300 cal BP. The 50% curves remain closer to the baseline because untreated sites contribute all dates. Youngest sampling produces the opposite and stronger edge effect: at 100% application density is concentrated in the late Holocene, with the three summaries peaking at 600-700 cal BP; at 50% the recent rise is amplified more modestly.

The bracketed rule reweights curves towards both observed site endpoints and gives an intermediate deformation. Random-1 and random-5 preserve the broad baseline shape, but reducing every site to one random date produces much wider conditional envelopes; random-5 envelopes are narrower. Within each scenario, SPD, KDE, and FD usually track one another despite their different boundary membership. For the full-site oldest and youngest rules, deformation from the all-dates reference is much larger than the baseline differences among summary methods. We therefore tested analogous rules in simulations where the generating population trajectory was known.

### 3.2 Simulation experiments

Figures 3-5 report the curve-expectation production results under the $\ell^1$ likelihood; $\ell^2$ gave the same broad ranking (Supplementary Information). Each accuracy and support estimate pools 1,000 replicates from each generating family ($n = 3{,}000$); the all-dates reference has no sub-sampling intensity and is repeated across the intensity axis for comparison.

#### 3.2.1 Model recovery

Model recovery is summarised in Figure 3. The all-dates and multi-date random strategies retain high accuracy across all three intensities. The all-dates reference recovers the generating family in 0.965 of replicates, while random-5 remains between 0.952 and 0.956. Random-3 is slightly less accurate, declining from 0.954 at 50% application to 0.917 at 100%. Reducing each treated site to one random date produces a larger monotonic decline, from 0.947 to 0.833.

The oldest and youngest rules fare poorly. Oldest sampling falls from 0.922 accuracy at 50% application to 0.333 at 100%. Youngest sampling begins at 0.388 and reaches 0.333 by 75%. The bracketed rule remains between 0.648 and 0.666: at 75% it lies below oldest but above youngest, and at 100% it exceeds both, but it does not match random sampling of three or five dates. At 100% intensity, oldest always selects the uniform family, youngest always selects the exponential family, and bracketed selects exponential for both increasing trajectories. The pooled fractions therefore include deterministic classification collapse, not merely uncertain choices around chance.

#### 3.2.2 Parameter bias

Parameter estimates reveal the direction of these effects (Fig. 4). When the generating model is exponential, the growth rate is effectively unbiased under all dates and random-*n*. The oldest and youngest rules show strong, sign-opposed biases that increase with intensity: oldest underestimates the rate (negative bias), youngest overestimates it (positive bias), and bracketed inherits a milder averaged negative bias. Under linear truth, slopes behave similarly on a tighter scale. At 100% oldest or youngest sampling, estimates of the generating growth parameter saturate the prespecified fitting bounds, so the plotted extreme bias magnitudes are bounded rather than unconstrained.

#### 3.2.3 Unambiguous model support

Figure 5 distinguishes selecting the generating family by minimum AIC from supporting it without an equally plausible alternative. The all-dates reference gives the strongest unambiguous support (0.931). Random-5 declines modestly from 0.901 to 0.861 as application increases, while random-3 falls from 0.886 to 0.773. Random-1 declines more sharply, from 0.852 to 0.614, despite retaining 0.833 minimum-AIC accuracy at 100% application. Sparse random sampling therefore weakens discrimination among growth families before it produces frequent outright misclassification.

The biased rules again perform poorly. Unambiguous support under oldest sampling falls from 0.775 to 0.333, while youngest sampling falls from 0.438 to 0.333. Bracketed sampling remains almost constant at 0.667. At 75-100% intensity its pooled support exceeds either boundary alone, but it cannot distinguish the linear and exponential generating families at 100% intensity. Random sampling of three or five dates retains substantially stronger discrimination.

#### 3.2.4 Realised-assay sensitivity

The internally paired sensitivity covered all-dates, random-1, random-3, and random-5 only. Across model, intensity, strategy, and likelihood cells, realised assays changed accuracy by -1.5 to +0.8 percentage points and unambiguous support by -1.9 to +0.9 points. The original v1 full-family screen, which covered marginal arms and differences, remains FAIL (15 of 18 gates passed). A separately labelled, explicitly post-unblinding v2 diagnostic calibrated intervals only over 96 accuracy, 96 support, and 72 directional boundary differences. Its pooled/truth-specific endpoints were 0.0203/0.030, 0.024/0.038, and 0.008/0.008, within the corresponding margins. Independent 50,000-resample bootstrap seeds gave identical critical widths and decisions. v2 supports interpreting the shifts as small but does not replace or retroactively pass v1.

### 3.3 Synthesis

The empirical displays (Fig. 2) and conditional simulations (Figs 3-5) show that within-site selection can reshape both descriptive curves and model-based inference. In the simulations, greater site coverage cannot compensate for a poor rule: forcing every site into oldest, youngest, or random-1 regimes reduces accuracy, weakens model discrimination, or induces directional parameter bias. Retaining all dates or random samples of three or five dates maintains high recovery and negligible mean bias in the curve-expectation experiment; focused realised-assay point estimates and the v2 difference-aligned diagnostic preserved this ranking, although only 15 of 18 v1 gates passed. Bracketed sampling is not a general substitute: its minimum-AIC accuracy is lower than random-1 at every intensity, and its unambiguous support exceeds random-1 only at 100% application. Because simulated site membership does not encode occupation span, these results identify the effect of selection among exchangeable dates rather than temporal span as a mechanism.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Effects of within-site sampling

Our study measured how within-site sampling choices propagate into regional demographic inference from radiocarbon determinations. Within the implemented normalised empirical displays, the choice among SPDs, FDs, and KDEs has a smaller visual effect than what gets dated within sites and how many samples are retained. In the conditional simulations, all-dates and multi-date random rules recover the generating family reliably, yield near-zero mean parameter bias, and sustain clearer support over alternatives. Oldest, youngest, and random-1 rules reduce performance in different ways. These outcomes arise in an idealised archive where research intensity and taphonomy are held constant and dates are independent within sites. The production analysis used calibration-curve expectations; focused realised-assay shifts were small and met the post-unblinding difference-aligned diagnostic margins, while the more conservative original full-family screen remains FAIL.

The AustArch panels show edge-selection effects without requiring historical interpretation. The oldest rule shifts mass earlier by approximating a distribution of site onsets; the youngest rule changes the opposite edge; bracketed sampling reweights curves toward both endpoints; and random-1 inflates between-realisation variance. The simulations quantify the consequences of applying analogous rules to exchangeable draws, but they do not show that occupation span itself explains the ranking. Increasing site coverage cannot compensate for a biased within-site rule. It can instead make biased estimates look more precise.

### 4.2 Implications for dates-as-data methods

These results add a dataset-formation layer to existing debates about SPDs, KDEs, FDs, and model-based approaches [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @brown2015FilterDarklyPopulation; @carleton2021SumThingsAre; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @crema2016SummedProbabilityDistribution; @riris2018DatesDataRevisited; @shennan2007PrehistoricPopulationHistory; @smith2016UseSummedprobabilityPlots; @surovell2007NoteUseTemporal; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. Recent count-based and Bayesian methods improve the treatment of chronological uncertainty and dated-event intensity once data are assembled [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @hanna2025SumPoissonApproach; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon]. Our results identify a prior source of bias: if many sites contribute only their earliest context, latest context, or a single arbitrary context, the regional evidence is compromised before any curve is drawn or model is fitted.

The likelihood analysis used here separates descriptive smoothing from demographic inference. SPDs, KDEs, and FDs remain useful for exploration and communication, but the demographic question is asked at the level of explicit growth models fitted to calibrated posteriors. This complements work showing that radiocarbon curves can diverge from other proxies when the underlying sampling process differs [@roalkvam2025ComparingSummedProbability]. It also extends population-growth modelling debates around SPD inputs and likelihood construction [@freeman2018CultureProcessInterpretation; @timpson2015InferentialMistakesPopulation; @timpson2021DirectlyModellingPopulation; @torfing2015NeolithicPopulationSummed]. The parsimony rule makes weak discrimination visible rather than reducing every replicate to one winning family. The same framework could be extended to richer growth families, including multi-phase or changepoint models.

### 4.3 Implications for legacy datasets, study design and repositories

Large aggregated samples are often treated as sufficient to dilute local idiosyncrasies [see @timpson2014ReconstructingRegionalPopulation; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon], but this is unsafe when the same within-site bias is repeated across many sites. If site contributions are systematically oldest or youngest, adding more sites can make the biased result look more precise. The simulations support auditing selection rules and retaining multiple determinations, but random-*n* is a representative-sampling benchmark rather than a literal field prescription. Because the simulated sites have no occupation histories, the analysis does not compare alternative stratigraphic designs or establish how many well-spaced contexts are sufficient.

The risk is greatest in legacy datasets assembled when dating was expensive and one- or two-date sites were common. This does not make previous regional or continental models invalid, nor does it imply that sparsely dated sites should be discarded. It does mean that regional analyses should audit site-level date counts and test whether results change when one- and two-date sites are excluded, down-weighted, or analysed separately. Datasets with more determinations per site are likely to be less vulnerable to single-date selection, but sparse sites can still matter if they cluster in particular regions, periods, materials, or site types [@bird2022P3k14cSyntheticGlobal; @loftus2019ArchaeologicalRadiocarbonDatabase; @lucarini2020MedAfriCarbonRadiocarbonDatabase; @saktura2023SahulArchGeochronologicalDatabase].

For new work, date all eligible secure contexts where budgets allow. Otherwise, distributing multiple determinations across the occupational sequence is a prudent design heuristic, although stratified sampling was not tested here and no universal minimum follows from the simulations. Databases should record site-level date counts (the number of radiocarbon determinations each site contributes), stratigraphic span, and, where known, the selection logic behind dated contexts. Recent large-scale data-compilation and rescaling approaches improve inter-site standardisation and sampling balance [@bird2022P3k14cSyntheticGlobal; @bunbury2025RobustDemographicModels; @loftus2019ArchaeologicalRadiocarbonDatabase; @lucarini2020MedAfriCarbonRadiocarbonDatabase; @park2026RescalingRadiocarbonData], but within-site selection rules also need to be visible if analysts are to distinguish accuracy from confidence.

### 4.4 Limitations and future directions

Our experiments held taphonomy and research intensity constant to isolate within-site selection, but real archives combine all three processes [@becerra-valdivia2020ChallengesSampleProcessing; @bluhm2019ValidationGlobalModel; @contreras2024LandscapeTaphonomyPredictably; @peros2010PrehistoricDemographyNorth; @shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @surovell2009CorrectingTemporalFrequency; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. Within-site selection can therefore compound the pull of recent samples or suppress genuine early peaks, and taphonomic correction applied to a biased evidence base may compound error. A defensible workflow should first audit within-site selection and coverage, then fit population models to calibrated posteriors, and finally test how taphonomic or research-intensity adjustments alter the inference.

The simulations deliberately isolate three growth families over a fixed Holocene window and treat dates as independent within exchangeable sites. They omit spatial heterogeneity, occupation histories, material-specific offsets, context-selection processes, and correlated calibration-curve error. Figures 3-5 condition conventional ages on the calibration-curve expectation; the paired sensitivity added independent realised assay draws only for all-dates, random-1, random-3, and random-5. Its post-unblinding difference-aligned diagnostic met its margins, but the original full-family screen did not pass every gate; oldest, youngest, and bracketed rules remain untested under realised noise. Under 100% oldest or youngest sampling, growth estimates reached fitting bounds, censoring extreme bias. Marine determinations lacked site-specific $\Delta R$ corrections. Pleistocene applications add stronger taphonomic loss, fewer well-dated sites, wider calibration uncertainty, and larger offsets. Future work should add site-duration structure, stratified sampling, correlated curve uncertainty, richer demographic histories, and bias-aware estimators while fitting calibrated determinations rather than smoothed curves [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @crema2017SpatiotemporalApproachesArchaeological; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @demjan2016ModellingDistributionArchaeological; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon].

### 4.5 Conclusions

Within-site sampling belongs at the start, not the end, of radiocarbon-based demographic and environmental analysis. SPDs, KDEs, and FDs remain useful descriptive summaries, but they should not carry the full inferential burden. Regional syntheses should audit per-site date counts, document selection rules, and test sensitivity to sparse sites, especially in legacy datasets. Field programmes should retain multiple secure contexts where possible, while recognising that the optimal number and spacing of dates depend on site histories and were not established here. What a “dates-as-data” model can recover depends on how radiocarbon dates are analysed and how the dated contexts entered the dataset.
