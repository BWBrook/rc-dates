# Within-site date-retention rules can distort radiocarbon “dates-as-data” inference

## Abstract

Choosing which radiocarbon dates a site contributes can change the regional history inferred from them. We tested this effect using 540 charcoal or wood determinations from 68 AustArch sites in Australia, and simulations in which the true regional history was constant, increasing or decreasing through time. Keeping every site's oldest date shifted the mean of the empirical summed probability distribution about 3,100 years older, whereas keeping one random date per site shifted it about 100 years, with a central 95% range of −433 to +594 years. Oldest and youngest retention changed temporal shape more than the choice among summed-probability, frequency-distribution and kernel-density summaries in all nine sensitivity configurations. In the simulations, retaining only the oldest date from each site produced inferred decline, and retaining only the youngest produced growth, in all 1,000 replicates of every tested history. Either rule converted a constant history into apparent exponential change and reversed a history changing in the opposing direction. Random retention preserved the direction of the simulated ten-fold changes, although fewer dates reduced discrimination between linear and exponential trajectories. Retaining both extrema preserved direction but converted every linear trajectory to an exponential selection when applied to all sites. The simulations used independent regional dates, without realised measurement noise or site occupation histories. Regional dates-as-data analyses should report how each site's dates were selected and test whether their conclusions survive plausible alternative retention rules.

## Keywords

radiocarbon dating; palaeodemography; dataset formation; date retention; order statistics; model selection; simulation.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Radiocarbon dates as population evidence

Archaeologists aggregate radiocarbon determinations to investigate temporal change in human activity and, by proxy, population size [@rick1987DatesDataExamination; @robinson2019SpatiotemporalDynamicsPrehistoric; @ulm1996RadiocarbonCulturalChronologies; @williams2016RadiometricDatesAre]. The premise is that, other things being equal, more people leave more evidence of dateable activity, so the frequency of dates may track changes in population size. This “dates-as-data” logic has been applied to European Neolithic demography [@shennan2013RegionalPopulationCollapse; @timpson2014ReconstructingRegionalPopulation], Holocene Sahul [@johnson2011ReconstructingDynamicsAncient; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon; @williams2013NewPopulationCurve], other regional sequences [@brown2019MaoriPopulationGrowth; @chaput2016RadiocarbonDatesEstimates; @porcic2016DemographyEarlyNeolithic] and continental syntheses [e.g., @wirtz2024MulticentennialCyclesContinental]. Each determination enters these analyses as a calibrated age, a probability distribution over calendar years that is often multimodal because atmospheric radiocarbon has varied through time. Common representations include summed probability distributions (SPDs), which add these distributions together, as well as frequency distributions (FDs), kernel density estimates (KDEs) and explicit population or occupancy models [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @brown2015FilterDarklyPopulation; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @mendez-quiros2023ModelingLongtermHuman; @michczynska2007FrequencyDistributionRadiocarbon; @robinson2019SpatiotemporalDynamicsPrehistoric].

The link between dates and people is conditional. Differential preservation produces a “pull of the recent”, because younger deposits are more likely to survive [@bluhm2019ValidationGlobalModel; @surovell2009CorrectingTemporalFrequency]. Research effort also varies through space and time, and inter-settlement sampling intensity can affect regional reconstructions [@park2026RescalingRadiocarbonData]. Calibration, binning, smoothing and model choice add further sensitivity [@carleton2021SumThingsAre; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric]. These problems have motivated taphonomic adjustments, binning protocols, simulation tests and models that treat dated events as count or point processes rather than smoothed densities [@crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon; @peros2010PrehistoricDemographyNorth; @williams2015HoloceneDemographicChanges]. Yet all such methods inherit the set of determinations supplied to them.

### 1.2 Within-site retention as dataset formation

Regional databases contain unequal numbers of dates per site, an imbalance that reflects budgets, preservation, research priorities and the contexts selected for dating [@codding2024CountsRadiocarbondatedArchaeological]. In the original AustArch compilation, 53% of sites had one determination and 86% had fewer than four [@williams2014AustArchDatabase14C]. These counts raise a question that date totals alone cannot answer: how was a site's contribution chosen? A single determination may be the only sample dated, or the only date retained from a larger set, and either selection history matters when that date is used as evidence of regional activity.

Prospective selection of material expected to lie near the beginning or end of a layer has long been recognised as violating random-thinning assumptions in chronological models [@nicholls2001RadiocarbonDatingTemporal]. Here we isolate a different process: selection among completed determinations. Repeatedly retaining a site's oldest or youngest date changes which part of its age distribution enters the regional dataset. In statistical terms, the retained values are extreme order statistics, whose distribution differs systematically from that of the dates they were drawn from. Of five ages drawn from a constant distribution, for example, the oldest falls in the oldest fifth of the range about two times in three, against one in five for a random date.

Choosing a basal context before dating is not equivalent to post hoc retention, and an extreme calibrated determination is not necessarily an occupation onset or termination. Nor does rescaling site contributions to adjust for unequal sampling intensity [@park2026RescalingRadiocarbonData] correct age-dependent choice among a site's dates. Our experiment quantifies the effect of post hoc retention separately from these prospective and analytical choices, combining an AustArch benchmark [@williams2014AustArchDataset; @williams2014AustArchDatabase14C] with simulations in which the regional trajectory is known. The empirical analysis compares oldest and youngest retention with one randomly retained date per site, and asks whether retention changes temporal shape more than choosing among SPD, FD and KDE summaries of identical dates. The simulations then test whether retention can create an apparent trend from a constant history or reverse the direction of a changing one.

## 2. Methods

Detailed filters, equations and reproducibility records are given in the Supplementary Information.

### 2.1 Empirical benchmark

We began with a 976-determination, 116-site Holocene reference set derived from the Archaeology Data Service AustArch file `Austarch_1-3_and_IDASQ_28Nov13-1.csv`; the derivation and its provenance are documented in the Supplementary Information and repository [@williams2014AustArchDataset; @williams2014AustArchDatabase14C]. For the primary empirical benchmark, we retained terrestrial charcoal or wood records coded as occupation-related (`Y`) or of uncertain occupation relation (`U`), excluding shell and human bone. All retained dates were calibrated with the Southern Hemisphere curve SHCal20 [@hogg2020SHCal20SouthernHemisphere]. We then required a median of the full calibrated posterior within 751–11,550 calendar years before present (cal BP; present = AD 1950), at least 0.80 posterior mass within the 651–11,650 cal BP analysis window, and at least five eligible determinations per site. The resulting set contained 540 dates from 68 sites: 181 coded `Y` and 359 coded `U`. Because exactly these dates entered every summary method, we call it the common-membership benchmark. By design it is restricted to well-replicated sites and is not an unbiased sample of Sahul occupation.

We constructed three unit-integral summaries on common 100-year bins. The SPD summed each calibrated posterior within the analysis window and applied a centred 100-year running mean, the FD binned posterior medians with a two-bin running mean for display, and the KDE placed Gaussian kernels of 100-year bandwidth on the same medians, reflected at both window boundaries to limit edge bias. Every method received the same determinations in every realisation. The curves describe relative temporal shape, because normalisation removes information about the number of retained dates. They serve only to describe the empirical benchmark; in the simulations, models are fitted to individual calibrated age distributions, not to the SPD, FD or KDE curves.

### 2.2 Empirical retention experiment

We compared all dates with five post hoc retention rules: random-1 and random-5 (one or five dates chosen at random from each treated site), oldest, youngest and bracketed (oldest plus youngest). A rule was applied to 50% or 100% of sites; untreated sites retained all eligible dates. Random retention and random selection of half the sites used 1,000 deterministic-seed realisations, whereas full-site oldest, youngest and bracketed scenarios were deterministic. Extrema were defined by the median of the full calibrated posterior, with posterior mean and then stable record identifier breaking ties. A median summarises a posterior that may be multimodal, so “oldest” and “youngest” are rules about calibrated summaries, not claims about the true age order within a site.

Figure 2 plots the difference between each retained curve and that method's all-date baseline; its pointwise 2.5th–97.5th percentile envelopes describe conditional realisations, not uncertainty over the archive or population history. We quantified curve separation using total variation,

$$
D_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_t |p_t-q_t|.
$$

Here $p_t$ and $q_t$ are the normalised probability masses in bin $t$, and $D_{\mathrm{TV}}$ is the share of mass that must be moved to turn one curve into the other: zero for identical curves and one for non-overlapping curves. For each realisation, a conservative contrast $C$ subtracted the largest between-method distance on the retained dates from the smallest method-specific retention distance to its all-date baseline. Retention was judged to matter more than the choice of method only when $C$ was positive (for stochastic scenarios, when its 2.5th percentile was positive): that is, when even the least-affected method had moved further from its own baseline than any two methods differed on the same retained dates. Mean-cal-BP shifts measured temporal displacement; positive values are older. Prespecified sensitivities varied the calendar buffer, posterior-mass threshold, KDE bandwidth and boundary correction, and restricted the benchmark to `Y` records.

### 2.3 Simulation of constant and changing histories

The simulation used the 976-date, 116-site reference to estimate a truncated negative-binomial distribution of dates per site and a regression of logged reported error on radiocarbon age. Each replicate contained 116 site labels with at least five dates per site. Calendar ages were drawn independently from one of five regional event-age densities over 651–11,650 cal BP, each describing the relative temporal distribution of dated events and integrating to one: uniform, linear increase, linear decrease, exponential increase or exponential decrease (Supplementary Fig. S1). Each non-uniform truth had a ten-fold end-to-end ratio. We summarised every trajectory by the log ratio of density at the recent and old ends of the window,

$$
\theta=\log\!\left\{\frac{f(651)}{f(11650)}\right\}.
$$

Here “increase” means greater density towards the present, at the low-cal-BP end, so increasing, decreasing and uniform truths had $\theta=\log(10)$, $-\log(10)$ and 0, respectively. Sites were labels only: they determined the number of dates and subsequent retention but imposed no occupation windows, stratigraphy or within-site dependence.

The calibration-curve-mean observation model mapped each calendar age to the SHCal20 expected conventional age, simulated a reported one-sigma error from the fitted age–error relation, and recalibrated it. Each simulated determination therefore carried a quoted error drawn from the empirical model and the full calibrated-posterior shape, but its central conventional age was the curve mean at the true calendar age, with no added measurement scatter. Realised scatter was examined only in an earlier paired sensitivity, restricted to constant and increasing histories and to the all-dates, random-1, random-3 and random-5 rules; its prespecified assessment and subsequent diagnostic are reported in Supplementary Section S3.

We compared seven retention rules with an all-dates reference: random-1, random-2, random-3, random-5, oldest, youngest and bracketed, with extrema selected by posterior median. Each rule treated 50%, 75% or 100% of sites, and untreated sites retained all dates. We generated 1,000 replicates for every truth-by-proportion combination, with common random numbers applying every rule to the same simulated dates within a replicate.

We fitted three functional families (uniform, linear and exponential) directly to the retained calibrated posteriors. Both non-uniform families allowed increase or decrease. For date $i$, the primary marginal log likelihood was the log of the candidate density averaged over the date's calibrated age distribution,

$$
\ell_i=\log\int f(x\mid\theta)p_i(x)\,dx,
$$

where $p_i(x)$ is the calibrated posterior. Calibration used a uniform calendar-age prior; each posterior was restricted to the strict interior of the 651–11,650 cal BP analysis window and renormalised before fitting. Conditional on posterior mass within that window, $\ell_i$ is the observed-data marginal log likelihood up to a date-specific additive constant independent of $\theta$; these constants cancel in within-dataset AIC comparisons [@timpson2021DirectlyModellingPopulation]. Minimum Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) selected the family [@akaike1974NewLookStatistical]. Because AIC penalises each fitted parameter, a linear or exponential family was preferred to the uniform only when its improvement in fit outweighed that penalty.

This criterion treats retained dates as independent regional draws and does not model their retention probabilities. Under bracketed retention, the two extrema retained from a site are dependent, but the product criterion treats them as if they were independent (working independence); bracketed AIC and family-selection results therefore describe the analysed workflow rather than likelihood support under a joint paired-order-statistic model. A separate $\ell^2$ analysis used the posterior expectation of the model log density; we call this an expected-log-density scoring sensitivity, not an ordinary likelihood analysis.

We measured correct-direction selection (uniform for a constant history, otherwise the correct direction), selection of the generating family, and the fitted generating-family endpoint contrast. For Figure 4, we used the bounded scale

$$
c=\frac{f(651)-f(11650)}{f(651)+f(11650)}=\tanh(\theta/2).
$$

This contrast ranges from −1 to 1, remains defined when one endpoint density is zero, and equals $\pm9/11$ for the ten-fold truths. It describes relative endpoint density, not a growth rate or recovery of the full density shape. We transformed each fitted replicate before calculating means and central 95% replicate ranges, and show the fraction near an original parameter bound for every group (Supplementary Section S2.4). With 1,000 replicates, the largest possible Monte Carlo standard error for a selection rate is 0.016.

### 2.4 Reproducibility

Analyses used R 4.5.3, rcarbon and a pinned package environment [@crema2021InferenceLargeSets; @rcoreteam2025LanguageEnvironmentStatistical]. Deterministic seeds make the simulations and random retention repeatable, and checkpoints, source fingerprints and checksum manifests link the accepted evidence to its generating code. The review archive provides code, aggregate empirical evidence and synthetic simulation outputs; source archaeological records are governed separately (Data availability).

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Oldest and youngest retention moved the empirical curves by millennia

Retaining one date per site had very different consequences depending on how that date was chosen (Fig. 2). Keeping each site's oldest date shifted the SPD mean 3,133 years older, and keeping the youngest shifted it 2,439 years younger. Keeping one random date per site shifted the mean by +103 years on average across 1,000 realisations, with a central 95% range of −433 to +594 years. All three rules retained 68 dates, and their SPD total-variation distances from the 540-date baseline were 0.455 for oldest and 0.524 for youngest, compared with a random-1 mean of 0.204 (95% range 0.158–0.256). These comparisons distinguish age-based selection from retained count; the ranges describe repeated retention from this fixed benchmark.

By comparison, the three all-date summaries had similar broad temporal structure (Fig. 1), with distances of 0.046 for SPD–KDE, 0.088 for FD–KDE and 0.101 for SPD–FD. When oldest or youngest retention was applied to every site, its deformation exceeded between-method differences under the prespecified conservative contrast: $C=0.193$ and $0.370$, respectively. This ordering and the direction of the mean shift held in all nine sensitivity configurations, including the 154-date, 24-site occupation-related (`Y`-only) subset.

Treating half the sites produced smaller changes. Mean distances across SPD, FD and KDE were 0.092–0.120 for oldest retention and 0.101–0.130 for youngest, with SPD mean shifts of +349 years (95% range −22 to +738) and −276 years (−673 to +137), respectively. Neither half-site rule met the retention-versus-method criterion, and nor did random retention at either treatment proportion or half-site bracketed retention.

Retaining both extrema from every site gave a smaller temporal displacement: the SPD mean shifted +352 years, within the random-1 shift range, while its distance was 0.279, above random-1's 97.5th percentile of 0.256. Bracketed retention kept 136 dates, twice as many as random-1, so random-1 is not a count-matched test for this rule, and the empirical experiment has no random-2 comparator. Across methods, bracketed distances were 0.279–0.326, and the conservative contrast was positive in eight of the nine configurations, including the primary analysis ($C=0.084$), but negative in the `Y`-only sensitivity ($C=-0.054$).

### 3.2 The retention rule controlled the inferred direction

The simulations show how retention alone can create an apparent regional trend (Fig. 3). From a constant generating history, retaining every site's oldest date selected exponential decline in all 1,000 replicates; retaining the youngest selected exponential growth in all 1,000. The same rules always imposed decline and growth, respectively, on the four changing histories, so a rule opposing the generating trend reversed it in every replicate.

This complete reversal occurred only when every site was treated. At 50% and 75% treatment, the opposing rule never reversed a changing history, although it increasingly selected a constant one instead. Constant histories were more sensitive to partial treatment: oldest and youngest retention selected a directional model in 97.3% and 97.9% of replicates at 50%, and their respective exponential directions in every replicate from 75%. All four random rules preserved the direction of every changing history at every treatment proportion. Bracketed retention also preserved those directions and selected uniform in 999 of 1,000 constant-history replicates when every site was treated.

Fitted endpoint contrasts show the distortion in relative density (Fig. 4). With all dates and random-5, mean generating-family contrasts remained close to the true $|c|=9/11\approx0.818$. When every site was treated, an opposing oldest or youngest rule reversed the fitted sign, and a rule aligned with the generating trend drove contrasts towards an endpoint. For linear fits, the natural parameter boundary gives zero density at one end and $c=\pm1$, so the corresponding log density ratio is infinite; exponential fits instead have an imposed finite rate cap. Even random-1 produced near-boundary linear fits in 7.2% and 8.2% of replicates under increasing and decreasing truths, respectively, so finite log-ratio means at the linear boundary would depend on optimiser tolerance. Bracketed retention attenuated the mean absolute contrast to 0.345–0.351 under linear histories and 0.585–0.597 under exponential histories.

### 3.3 Correct direction does not ensure correct shape

Selecting the direction of change was easier than distinguishing linear from exponential change (Fig. 5). With all dates, generating-family selection rates were 0.974–0.976 for linear histories, 0.996–0.998 for exponential histories and 0.848 for constant histories. Even with a constant generating history, AIC can favour an extra parameter, and it does not guarantee selection of the generating model. Across the changing histories and treatment proportions, random retention preserved direction while generating-family selection ranged from 0.754 to 0.995 as dates were discarded.

Bracketed retention illustrates the distinction. Applied to every site, it selected exponential in every replicate generated from either linear or exponential histories, so preserving the direction concealed a complete loss of linear-family recovery. Likewise, its 999 uniform selections under a constant history cannot establish shape recovery, because the candidate set contains no U-shaped alternative. The choice of scoring rule mattered little: the primary marginal-likelihood and expected-log-density analyses agreed on the selected family and direction in 99.75% of 120,000 paired selections, including every all-site oldest and youngest result.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Order-statistic distortion under exchangeability

The direction inferred from a regional date distribution can follow the selection rule rather than the generating history. Selecting the oldest observation from each site constructs a distribution of maxima, and selecting the youngest a distribution of minima. The mechanism follows directly from order statistics: for $n$ independent, exactly observed ages with cumulative distribution $F(x)$, the oldest has cumulative distribution $F(x)^n$ and the youngest $1-\{1-F(x)\}^n$, so a site with ten dates tends to contribute a more extreme oldest date than one with five. In the simulations, these filters manufactured exponential selections from constant histories and reversed trends that ran against the retained extreme.

Pooling both extrema does not restore the original distribution. For uniform ages, their equally weighted density is U-shaped whenever $n\geq3$, with too many dates near both ends of the range (Supplementary Section S2.6). A uniform model can nevertheless win against monotonic alternatives, which is why correct direction or family selection is insufficient evidence of shape recovery.

Partial treatment mixes unequal contributions. Under an oldest or youngest rule a treated site contributes one date and an untreated site at least five, so at 50% or 75% site treatment the filtered sites supply a much smaller share of retained dates than the treatment percentage suggests. The absence of reversals in those scenarios describes that mixture, rather than a general safe threshold.

The AustArch benchmark makes the size of the effect tangible. At the same retained count, oldest and youngest retention moved the mean by millennia, compared with about a century under random-1. Bracketed retention is harder to judge: its mean shift falls within the random-1 range while its SPD total variation exceeds that range, and a random-2 benchmark would be needed to assess it at the same date count.

### 4.2 Consequences for dates-as-data analysis

Accounting for calibration uncertainty alone does not correct age-dependent retention. The regional-density fits tested here use full calibrated posteriors, yet assume that the retained dates are a representative sample of the regional distribution. A model representing a known selection process could address this bias, but a better likelihood cannot substitute for examining how the dataset was assembled.

Dataset formation belongs alongside debates about taphonomy, binning, rescaling and population models [@bronkramsey2017MethodsSummarizingRadiocarbon; @carleton2021SumThingsAre; @crema2022StatisticalInferencePrehistoric; @riris2018DatesDataRevisited; @surovell2007NoteUseTemporal; @williams2012UseSummedRadiocarbon]. Recent count-based and Bayesian approaches improve inference once a dataset is assembled [@arthur2023ProbabilisticReconstructionPaleodemographic; @crema2025BayesianAlternativeAoristic; @denavascues2025AnalysisAbundanceRadiocarbon; @heaton2025NewApproachRadiocarbon], but the audit we propose comes earlier. It should report per-site contributions, distinguish sites with few dated samples from sites with few retained dates, and record whether inclusion depended on age. Where the selection history is unknown, analyses can test plausible alternatives and identify which conclusions depend on them.

Sparse sites can remain useful evidence, but their material, period, region and selection history should accompany their numerical contribution [@bird2022P3k14cSyntheticGlobal; @loftus2019ArchaeologicalRadiocarbonDatabase; @lucarini2020MedAfriCarbonRadiocarbonDatabase; @saktura2023SahulArchGeochronologicalDatabase]. An oldest-date summary assembled to study arrival, for example, targets a different quantity from the distribution of dated activity through time, and reusing it for dates-as-data inference would carry its selection rule into the new analysis. This is a distinction between inferential targets, not a claim about how frequently such reuse occurs.

### 4.3 Limitations and next tests

The simulations isolate post hoc retention among independent regional ages. Site labels determine date counts and retention, without occupation durations, stratigraphy, spatial structure or context-selection processes. The changing histories span a ten-fold endpoint contrast, and weaker or non-monotonic signals may behave differently, so the observed success of random retention provides neither a universal dating minimum nor an optimal allocation of a fixed dating budget. Calibration-curve errors were not modelled as correlated, and bracketed fits use working independence. The bounded contrasts in Figure 4 also compress large endpoint ratios. Within the simulation, however, oldest and youngest are count-matched to random-1 and bracketed to random-2, so their differing outcomes cannot be attributed to retained count alone.

The observation model propagates calibrated-posterior width and curve structure but omits realised conventional-age variation. An earlier sensitivity found small changes for all-date and random rules: accuracy changed by −1.5 to +0.8 percentage points and unique generating-family support by −1.9 to +0.9 points. That historical workflow compared uniform against growth-only linear and exponential candidates, rather than the current candidates that allow change in either direction, and defined accuracy as selection of the generating family within that restricted competition. Its prespecified assessment met 15 of 18 criteria and remains failed; a subsequent post-unblinding diagnostic met its revised criteria (Supplementary Section S3). Neither analysis tested oldest, youngest or bracketed retention. If the same measurement process is applied independently to every date, a one-sided rule still retains an extreme of the noisy posterior-median distribution; whether AIC on the full posteriors then preserves the inferred direction and magnitude remains untested.

The empirical benchmark deliberately selects well-replicated charcoal or wood dates and includes records of uncertain occupation relation. Its normalised curves describe temporal shape rather than population abundance. The retention rules diagnose what can happen to this assembled database; they do not estimate the prevalence of those practices in existing archives or supply occupation onset and termination estimates. Site-history generators and comparisons of sampling allocations would be needed to extend the results to prospective archaeological sampling.

### 4.4 Conclusions

Which dates a site contributes can determine whether a regional analysis infers growth, decline or no change. In our simulations, retaining every site's oldest or youngest date created apparent trends from constant histories and reversed opposing ones, while retaining both extrema preserved direction but turned linear histories into exponential selections. The AustArch comparison shows why retained count alone is inadequate: equally sparse datasets had sharply different temporal shapes depending on which dates were kept. Regional analyses should make site contributions and retention rules explicit, then test whether their conclusions survive plausible changes to those rules.
